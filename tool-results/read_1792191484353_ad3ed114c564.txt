     3→Chapter 9 — Static Checking
     4→Where we are. Chapter 1 separated mistakes, faults, and failures, and promised that we attack defects at
     5→every stage. This chapter is about the family of techniques that find faults without running the code:
     6→architecture reviews, formal inspections, everyday code review, and automated static analysis. Chapter 10
     7→covers the complementary half — testing, which finds defects by running the code. Neither replaces the
     8→other, and a mature team leans on both.
     9→There is a persistent myth that the only way to know whether software works is to run it. Running it certainly
    10→helps, and Chapter 10 is devoted to doing so well. But a great deal of what is wrong with a program is visible on
    11→the page — a null that is never checked, a lock acquired but never released, an interface that two teams understand
    12→differently, an architecture that quietly assumes a database will never be slow. Static checking is the discipline of
    13→finding those problems by examining the software — its design documents, its source code, its types — rather
    14→than by executing it. The word “static” is the opposite of “dynamic”: nothing is running, no test input is chosen, no
    15→clock ticks.
    16→Why bother, when we could just write tests? Three reasons, each of which this chapter develops. First, static
    17→techniques find defects earlier, often before a single line of code exists — an architecture review can catch a
    18→scalability flaw during a design meeting, when fixing it costs an afternoon rather than a rewrite. Second, static
    19→techniques find defects testing cannot easily reach: a race condition that surfaces once in ten thousand runs, a
    20→security hole on an error path no test exercises, a misleading name that will cause a future bug. Third, static
    21→techniques transfer knowledge: when a senior engineer reviews your change, you both walk away understanding
    22→the system better, which no automated test can do. The through-line of the chapter is that reviews, inspection, and
    23→static analysis are complementary to testing, not substitutes for it, and that they operate on a spectrum from slow-
    24→and-human to fast-and-automated.
    25→9.1 Architecture Reviews
    26→The earliest and cheapest place to catch a defect is before it is built. An architecture review is a structured
    27→examination of a system’s design — its major components, their responsibilities, and the connections between
    28→them (Chapters 6–7) — conducted by people other than the designers. The goal is to stress the diagram, not
    29→admire it: to ask the questions that the design’s authors, being close to their own work, have stopped asking.
    30→Architecture reviews matter because architectural mistakes are usually the most expensive kind. A poorly named
    31→variable is a five-minute fix in code review. A decision to route every request through a single synchronous
    32→service, or to store money as a floating-point number, or to couple two modules that should have been
    33→independent, can require months of rework once the system is built around it. These decisions are made early, are
    34→
    35→===== صفحة الكتاب 233 =====
    36→hard to reverse, and constrain everything downstream — so they deserve a dedicated review before code is
    37→written.
    38→9.1.1 Guiding Principles for Architecture Reviews
    39→A few principles keep an architecture review productive rather than performative.
    40→Review against concrete scenarios, not in the abstract. “Is this a good design?” has no answer. “How
    41→does this design handle a burst of 10,000 concurrent users?” or “What happens when the payment service is
    42→down for thirty seconds?” has an answer you can evaluate. Bring the system’s quality requirements — its
    43→expected load, its latency budget, its failure modes — and walk the design through each one.
    44→The authors should not run the room. People defend their own decisions instinctively. A review where
    45→the designer both presents and judges is a monologue with extra steps. Someone else should facilitate, and
    46→the designer’s job is to explain and listen, not to win.
    47→Focus on the risky and the irreversible. Review time is scarce. Spend it on the decisions that are hard to
    48→change and costly if wrong — data models, trust boundaries, concurrency, external dependencies — not on
    49→choices that a later refactor can fix cheaply.
    50→End with recorded decisions, assigned owners, and follow-up actions. A review that ends with “good
    51→points, we’ll think about it” has failed. Every significant concern should end as a recorded item with an
    52→owner and a disposition: fix now, accept the risk (and why), or investigate further by a date.
    53→Principle. The purpose of an architecture review is to surface risks and assumptions while they are still
    54→cheap to change. If your review only confirms what the designers already believed, you scheduled it too late
    55→or invited the wrong people.
    56→Pitfall. Reviewing an architecture with only its authors in the room. They share the same blind spots — that
    57→is precisely why they didn’t catch the problem. Invite an engineer from a different team, someone who has
    58→operated a similar system in production, and someone who will have to integrate with this one.
    59→9.1.2 Discovery, Deep-Dive, and Retrospective Reviews
    60→Not all architecture reviews happen at the same time or ask the same questions. It helps to distinguish three
    61→moments in a system’s life, each with a different purpose.
    62→A discovery review happens early, when the design is still forming. Its aim is breadth: to map the problem space,
    63→name the major components, identify the big risks, and make sure no whole category of requirement (security,
    64→privacy, cost, operability) has been forgotten. A discovery review is exploratory and generative — you are trying
    65→to find the questions as much as answer them. The right output is a prioritized list of concerns and a set of deeper
    66→investigations to schedule.
    67→
    68→===== صفحة الكتاب 234 =====
    69→A deep-dive review happens once the shape is settled but a specific area carries outsized risk. Suppose the
    70→discovery review flagged the data-consistency model as the scariest part of the design. A deep-dive review takes
    71→that one area and examines it exhaustively: the exact failure scenarios, the concurrency interleavings, the
    72→migration path, the edge cases. Where a discovery review is a mile wide and an inch deep, a deep-dive is the
    73→reverse. You bring in specialists — a database expert, a security engineer — and you do not leave until the risk is
    74→either resolved or explicitly accepted.
    75→A retrospective review happens after the system is built and running, and asks a different question entirely: did
    76→the architecture actually deliver what we hoped? Where did reality diverge from the design? Which assumptions
    77→held and which broke? A retrospective review is about learning rather than blame, both to guide the next iteration
    78→of this system and to make the team better at designing. The Therac-25 disaster from Chapter 1 is, among other
    79→things, a story of a team that never conducted an honest retrospective on its own assumptions about software
    80→reliability.1
    81→The three are not mutually exclusive — a long-lived system will have all three, repeatedly. State which review you
    82→are running at the start: a deep-dive that turns into discovery loses its focus, while a discovery that demands deep-
    83→dive rigor exhausts everyone before the major risks are found.

    86→9.2 Conducting Software Inspections
    87→Where an architecture review examines a design, a software inspection examines a concrete work product —
    88→most often source code, but also a specification, a test plan, or a design document — line by line, with a defined
    89→process, for the explicit purpose of finding defects. Inspection is the most formal member of the static-checking
    90→family. It is slow and it is disciplined, and decades of data show that, done well, it is startlingly effective: teams
    91→routinely find more than half of a document’s defects in inspection, before any of that code runs.2
    92→The formal version was pioneered by Michael Fagan at IBM in the 1970s, and a Fagan inspection remains the
    93→archetype: a small group, a defined sequence of phases, assigned roles, and — crucially — measurement of the
    94→process itself.3 What distinguishes an inspection from someone “taking a look” is that it is repeatable and its
    95→effectiveness can be quantified.
    96→9.2.1 The Phases of a Traditional Inspection
    97→A traditional inspection moves through six phases.4 The discipline is in not skipping any of them, because each
    98→guards against a specific way that informal review fails.
    99→

   101→1. Planning. The author and moderator decide what will be inspected, confirm it meets entry criteria (it
   102→compiles, it is complete enough to review), choose the participants, and schedule the meeting. Planning
   103→also means keeping the chunk small — inspecting more than a few hundred lines in one sitting reliably
   104→degrades into skimming.5
   105→2. Overview. The author briefly educates the reviewers on the context: what this code is for, how it fits the
   106→larger system, any background they need. This phase is optional when reviewers already know the area, but
   107→omitting it when they don’t all but guarantees shallow review.
   108→3. Preparation. Each reviewer examines the work product individually and in advance, noting suspected
   109→defects and questions. This is the phase where most defects are actually found.6 The meeting exists to
   110→consolidate what individual, focused reading has already turned up, not to read the code for the first time.
   111→
   112→===== صفحة الكتاب 237 =====
   113→4. Meeting. The group convenes. The reader walks through the material (see the roles below), reviewers raise
   114→the issues they found in preparation, and the scribe records each confirmed defect. The critical rule: the
   115→meeting finds and records defects; it does not solve them. Debating fixes in the meeting is the classic way
   116→inspections run over time and lose their value — solutions are the author’s job during rework.
   117→5. Rework. The author takes the logged defect list away and fixes each item. Some “defects” turn out to be
   118→misunderstandings; the author notes the disposition of every one.
   119→6. Follow-up. The moderator verifies that every logged defect was actually addressed — not just that the
   120→author intended to. If the rework was substantial, or too many defects remain, the material may re-enter the
   121→process for another pass.
   122→Pitfall. Turning the inspection meeting into a redesign session. The moment the group starts brainstorming
   123→how to fix a defect rather than confirming that it is one, the meeting balloons, most participants disengage,
   124→and you cover a fraction of the material. Log the defect, move on, let the author solve it in rework.
   125→9.2.2 Case Study: Using Data to Ensure Effectiveness
   126→The feature that separates inspection from informal review is that inspection measures itself. Because the process
   127→is repeatable, you can collect numbers and use them to tell whether the process is healthy — and the numbers are
   128→often surprising.
   129→Case study. (A composite team, with illustrative numbers.) A team inspecting a mission-critical module
   130→tracks two quantities for every inspection: the preparation rate (lines of code each reviewer read per hour
   131→before the meeting) and the defect density (defects found per thousand lines). Over twenty inspections they
   132→notice a strong pattern: when reviewers prepare at 500 lines per hour or less, they find roughly 8 defects per
   133→thousand lines. When the preparation rate climbs above 1,000 lines per hour — reviewers skimming, under
   134→time pressure — the yield collapses to 2 per thousand. The defects did not vanish; the reviewers simply
   135→moved too fast to see them. Armed with this data, the team sets a policy: no inspection meeting proceeds
   136→unless every reviewer’s preparation rate was under 500 lines per hour. Their post-release defect rate on
   137→inspected modules drops by half.
   138→The lesson generalizes. A too-high preparation or review rate means reviewers are skimming, and a suspiciously
   139→low defect count is then bad news, not good — it usually means the review was shallow, not that the code was
   140→clean.5 By recording review rates, defect densities, and where in the lifecycle each defect was found, a team can
   141→answer questions that opinion alone cannot: Are our inspections worth the time? Which kinds of defects slip
   142→through? Should we inspect this class of module at all? Measurement turns inspection from a ritual into a
   143→controllable process, and connects directly to Chapter 12’s treatment of quality metrics.
   144→

   146→Principle. Slow down to speed up. In inspection, a lower review rate finds more defects, and finding a
   147→defect on the page is far cheaper than finding it in production.
   148→9.2.3 Organizing an Inspection
   149→An inspection works because the participants play distinct roles, not because more eyeballs are inherently better.7
   150→Assigning roles prevents the classic failure where everyone assumes someone else is doing the careful reading.
   151→Author. The person who wrote the work product. Their job in the meeting is to listen, answer questions of
   152→fact, and take defects away for rework — not to defend the code or argue it is fine. An author who treats the
   153→inspection as a trial to be won destroys it.
   154→Reviewer (inspector). The people whose job is to find defects. They do the essential preparation work
   155→individually beforehand and raise issues in the meeting. A good inspection has two to four reviewers with
   156→relevant expertise; many more than that and the meeting bogs down without finding proportionally more.3
   157→Reader. One reviewer designated to paraphrase the material aloud during the meeting — restating what
   158→each piece of code does in their own words, rather than reading it verbatim. This is subtly powerful: when
   159→the reader’s paraphrase diverges from what the author intended, you have found a place where the code
   160→does not say what it means, which is a defect (or a clarity problem that will cause a future defect).
   161→Moderator. The facilitator who keeps the meeting on process: enforcing the “find, don’t fix” rule, watching
   162→the pace, ensuring everyone prepared, and confirming follow-up. The moderator is deliberately not the
   163→author, so that no one is refereeing their own work.
   164→Scribe (recorder). The person who logs each confirmed defect precisely — location, description, severity
   165→— so that rework and follow-up have an unambiguous list to work from. A defect that isn’t written down
   166→didn’t happen.
   167→One person can sometimes hold two roles (the moderator often also scribes in a small team), but the author should
   168→never be the moderator or the reader of their own code — the entire value comes from other people examining it.
   169→If your “inspection” is just the author walking everyone through their own reasoning, you have recreated the very
   170→problem you were trying to escape.
   171→9.3 Code Reviews: Check Intent and Trust
   172→Full Fagan inspection is heavyweight, and most teams do not inspect every change that way — the cost is justified
   173→only for the riskiest material. What virtually every professional team does do, on nearly every change, is a lighter-
   174→weight code review: before a change is merged, at least one engineer other than the author reads it and must
   175→approve it. This is one of the most widely practiced static-checking techniques in the industry, and it is the daily
   176→face of everything the inspection literature discovered.8
   177→

   179→Modern code review through pull requests is less about hunting for every defect (automated tools, §9.4, are better
   180→at the mechanical ones) and more about two human questions that no tool can settle for you: intent and trust.8
   181→Intent: does this change do what the author meant it to do, and is what they meant actually the right thing? A
   182→reviewer who understands the system can see that a change is technically correct but solves the wrong problem, or
   183→introduces a subtle inconsistency with how the rest of the codebase works. Trust: code review is how a team
   184→collectively takes responsibility for its codebase. Once your change is approved and merged, it is not “your” code
   185→anymore — it is the team’s, and the reviewer who approved it has vouched for it. That shared ownership is the
   186→cultural payoff of review, and it is why “the reviewer will catch it” and “I’m just approving to unblock them” are
   187→both corrosive.
   188→Principle — repository stewardship. The primary question in a code review is not “is this how I would
   189→have written it?” but “will this change make the repository healthier over time?”9 Reviewers should identify
   190→risks and maintain standards without imposing personal preferences. If a change clearly improves the
   191→system, it should be approved even when the reviewer can imagine a marginally better version.
   192→This principle extends beyond the lines in the diff. A healthy change may also add a missing test, clarify
   193→nearby documentation, improve a misleading name, remove duplication, or strengthen the automation that
   194→verifies the code. The improvement should remain relevant and proportionate to the work at hand. Do not
   195→turn a focused feature or defect fix into an unrelated rewrite; when the cleanup is large, risky, or separable,
   196→record it and submit it as its own small change.
   197→9.3.1 Invested Expert Reviewers
   198→Not all reviewers are equal, and the difference is not raw skill — it is investment and context. The most valuable
   199→reviewer for a change is usually someone who knows that part of the system deeply and who has a stake in its
   200→long-term health: the person who will have to maintain it, who was burned by the last bug in this area, who
   201→understands the invariants that aren’t written down anywhere. An invested expert reviewer brings knowledge
   202→that is not in the diff — the history of why the code is shaped this way, the failure that a strange-looking guard
   203→clause exists to prevent, the plan for where this module is heading.
   204→This has practical consequences for how teams assign reviews. A change to the authentication system should be
   205→reviewed by someone who understands the security model, not by whoever happens to be free. Many teams
   206→encode this with code ownership: files or directories have designated owners whose approval is required for
   207→changes there, precisely to guarantee that an invested expert sees every change to sensitive code. It also means
   208→that as a reviewer, if you don’t have the context to evaluate a change — if you cannot tell whether it is correct —
   209→the honest move is to say so and route it to someone who can, not to rubber-stamp it. An approval from someone
   210→who didn’t understand the change provides trust that isn’t real, which is worse than no approval at all.

   213→9.3.2 Reviewing Is Done within Hours
   214→Modern practice also emphasizes review latency: teams should usually respond within hours rather than days.10
   215→Fast review supports the throughput of the whole development system, even though a slower review may appear
   216→more thorough in isolation.
   217→When a change sits unreviewed for days, several bad things happen. The author, blocked, either switches to other
   218→work (incurring the cost of context-switching back later) or, worse, starts building new changes on top of the
   219→unreviewed one, so a problem found in review now cascades. The change itself drifts out of date as the codebase
   220→moves under it, creating merge conflicts. And the author’s own memory of why they made each decision fades, so
   221→their responses to review comments get vaguer. Fast review keeps the author in flow, keeps changes small and
   222→current, and keeps the feedback loop tight enough that the author actually learns from it.
   223→“Within hours” does not mean “instantly and carelessly.” The practice that squares speed with quality is small
   224→changes: a reviewer can give a 50-line change a genuinely careful read in minutes and turn it around the same
   225→day, whereas a 2,000-line change cannot be reviewed well or quickly — the reviewer skims, approves out of
   226→fatigue, and the review becomes theater.5 Small, frequently reviewed changes are the mechanism that makes fast,
   227→thorough review possible at once. This is the everyday, industrialized descendant of the inspection data in §9.2.2:
   228→keep the chunk small, keep the reviewer engaged, and the defects surface.
   229→Pitfall. The giant pull request. A change touching forty files with 1,500 lines of diff cannot be reviewed with
   230→care — the reviewer’s attention is exhausted long before the end, so the last half gets a glance and a thumbs-
   231→up. If you want real review, break the work into small, independently reviewable changes. If you receive a
   232→giant PR, it is legitimate to ask for it to be split.
   233→9.4 Automated Static Analysis
   234→Human review is expensive and imperfect at mechanical checks — people are bad at reliably noticing that every
   235→code path closes a file handle, but computers are excellent at it. Automated static analysis is the use of tools that
   236→examine source code (or compiled artifacts) without running it and report likely defects, style violations, or
   237→dangerous patterns. These tools run in seconds on every change, never get tired, and never skip the boring parts —
   238→which is why they complement human review rather than competing with it. Let the machine catch the mechanical
   239→faults so the humans can spend their attention on intent and design.
   240→9.4.1 A Variety of Static Checkers
   241→“Static analysis” is an umbrella over several distinct kinds of tools, each answering a different question. It helps to
   242→know the categories so you can assemble the right set.
   243→

   245→Type checkers verify that operations are applied to compatible kinds of data — that you don’t add a
   246→number to a string, call a method that doesn’t exist, or pass an argument of the wrong type. In statically
   247→typed languages (Java, Rust, Go) the compiler is a type checker, and it rejects whole classes of defect
   248→before the program ever runs. Bolt-on type checkers bring the same guarantees to dynamically typed
   249→languages — for example, gradual type checkers for Python and JavaScript-family code. A type error
   250→caught at compile time is a failure that can never reach a user. Here a price arrives as a string and flows into
   251→arithmetic:
   252→In each fence, the trailing comment quotes its checker’s actual verdict — the same fault, caught before the
   253→code ever runs.
   254→Linters flag stylistic issues, suspicious constructs, and small correctness hazards: unused variables,
   255→shadowed names, missing break in a switch, comparison that is always true. Individually minor, these
   256→findings keep a codebase consistent and readable, and they catch the occasional real bug hiding as a typo.
   257→Linters are the cheapest static analysis to adopt and usually the first a team turns on.
   258→Data-flow analyzers trace how values move through a program to find defects that span multiple lines: a
   259→variable used before it is assigned, a resource opened on one path but not closed on another, a value that
   260→can be null reaching a dereference, tainted user input flowing into a database query (a SQL-injection risk).
   261→These are the checks humans are worst at, because they require mentally simulating every execution path
   262→— exactly what a machine is built to do. This exporter closes its file on the normal path but leaks it on the
   263→error path:

   265→return price * quantity

   274→fragile acquisition, a path-sensitive analyzer reporting the leaking path itself, or a runtime warning when
   275→the abandoned handle is finally collected. The acquisition and the early return are the guilty pair. They sit a
   276→few lines apart here and are often a screen or more apart in production code — a human tracing every path
   277→by eye misses the one that skips the close, while an analyzer walks each path mechanically.
   278→Bug-pattern finders encode a catalog of known-bad code shapes and scan for them: ignoring a method’s
   279→return value that must be checked, comparing strings with reference equality, integer overflow in a size
   280→calculation, a lock acquired without a matching release. Each pattern is a mistake that has bitten enough
   281→programmers that someone wrote a detector for it. Security-focused variants (often called SAST tools)
   282→specialize in patterns that lead to vulnerabilities — the security applications of static analysis get their own
   283→treatment, alongside dynamic and AI-driven testing, in Chapter 11.
   284→Most teams run several of these together in continuous integration (Chapters 2 and 14), so that every proposed
   285→change is automatically type-checked, linted, and pattern-scanned before a human reviewer ever looks at it. The
   286→tools handle the mechanical layer; the humans handle judgment. That division of labor is the whole point.
   287→9.4.2 False Positives and False Negatives
   288→Static analyzers are not oracles. Because they reason about all possible executions without running any of them,
   289→they must approximate, and approximation produces two kinds of error you must understand to use these tools
   290→well.
   291→A false positive is when the tool reports a defect that is not actually a defect — it cries wolf. The code is fine, but
   292→the analyzer, unable to prove it is fine, flags it anyway. A false negative is the opposite and more dangerous case:
   293→a real defect that the tool fails to report — the wolf slips past. The code is genuinely broken, but the analysis
   294→missed it, so you get false reassurance.
   295→def export_prices(catalog, discounts, path):

   298→for item, price in sorted(catalog.items()):

   300→if pct < 0 or pct > 100:
   301→return None
   302→# error path: `out` is never closed
   303→final = round(price * (1 - pct / 100), 2)
   304→out.write(f"{item},{final}\n")
   305→out.close()
   306→return path

   310→It is worth laying these out against the truth, in the same confusion-matrix form you will see again for testing and
   311→classification:
   312→Tool reports a defect
   313→Tool reports nothing
   314→Defect really exists
   315→True positive (correct alarm)
   316→False negative (missed defect)
   317→No defect exists
   318→False positive (false alarm)
   319→True negative (correctly quiet)
   320→The two error types trade off against each other, and this trade-off has names. Precision is the fraction of the tool’s
   321→alarms that are real: of everything it flagged, how much was an actual defect? Low precision means many false
   322→positives — lots of noise. Recall is the fraction of real defects the tool catches: of everything actually wrong, how
   323→much did it find? Low recall means many false negatives — lots of misses. You generally cannot maximize both
   324→at once. Make a tool more aggressive so it catches more real bugs (higher recall) and it will also flag more
   325→innocent code (lower precision). Tune it to only report what it is sure about (higher precision) and it will stay
   326→silent about defects it can’t prove (lower recall).
   327→Why does this trade-off decide whether a tool succeeds in practice? Because false positives have a human cost
   328→that compounds. If a tool floods reviewers with false alarms, people stop reading its output — and once ignored,
   329→it also misses the real defects buried in the noise. A checker with 90% recall is worthless if its 60% false-positive
   330→rate trains the whole team to click “dismiss” reflexively. This is why practical static-analysis tools often
   331→deliberately sacrifice recall for precision: a tool that reports fewer defects but is right almost every time earns the
   332→team’s trust and gets acted upon, while a “thorough” tool that is frequently wrong gets turned off. Google’s
   333→experience with static analysis is explicit about this — they target a false-positive rate low enough (roughly one in
   334→ten or better) that developers treat warnings as worth fixing rather than as noise.11
   335→Principle. For a static analyzer, trust is the scarce resource. A tool the team trusts gets acted upon; a tool the
   336→team distrusts gets suppressed, and then even its true findings are lost. High precision buys trust; chasing
   337→recall at the cost of precision spends it.
   338→Pitfall. Treating a clean static-analysis report as proof of correctness. False negatives are real: the analyzer
   339→only finds the categories of defect it was built to find, and says nothing about whether the code does the
   340→right thing. “The linter is happy” is a floor, not a ceiling — it is why static analysis complements, and never
   341→replaces, review and testing.
   342→9.5 Conclusion
   343→Every technique in this chapter finds defects without running the code, and each occupies a different point on a
   344→spectrum from slow-and-human to fast-and-automated. Architecture reviews catch the most expensive defects
   345→
   346→===== صفحة الكتاب 244 =====
   347→earliest, while the design is still cheap to change. Inspections apply disciplined, measured, role-based scrutiny to
   348→the riskiest work products and reliably find a large share of their defects before release. Code review is the
   349→everyday, industrialized version — fast, lightweight, focused on intent and shared ownership, and effective in
   350→direct proportion to how small the changes are. Automated static analysis handles the mechanical checks
   351→tirelessly and in seconds, freeing human attention for judgment, as long as we manage its false positives so the
   352→team keeps trusting it.
   353→These techniques are complementary to each other and, above all, complementary to testing (Chapter 10). Static
   354→checking excels at finding defects that are visible on the page, that lurk on rarely executed paths, and that concern
   355→design and intent — but it cannot tell you whether the assembled system actually behaves correctly when it runs.
   356→Testing can, but only for the inputs you think to try, and only after the code exists. The mistake → fault → failure
   357→chain from Chapter 1 is best broken at every link at once: reviews and inspection reduce the faults that reach the
   358→codebase, static analysis catches whole categories automatically, and testing provokes the failures that slip
   359→through. No single net catches everything, which is exactly why we cast several.

   388→Chapter 9 — Exercises
   389→Exercises are graded by depth: [warm‑up] checks understanding, [analysis] asks you to reason.
   390→Concepts
   391→1. [warm‑up] In one or two sentences each, explain what makes a technique static rather than dynamic, and
   392→give one example of each from this book. Then state the single biggest thing static checking cannot tell you
   393→that testing (Chapter 10) can.
   394→2. [warm‑up] Name the six phases of a traditional inspection in order, and give a one-sentence purpose for
   395→each. Which phase finds most of the defects, and why is it not the meeting?
   396→3. [warm‑up] List the five inspection roles (author, reviewer, reader, moderator, scribe) and, for each, state
   397→the one thing the inspection breaks down without. Explain why the author must never also be the moderator
   398→or reader of their own code.
   399→4. [warm‑up] Define false positive and false negative for a static analyzer in your own words, and give a
   400→concrete one-line example of each. Which one gives you false reassurance, and why is that the more
   401→dangerous error?

   410→dismissing its warnings without reading them. Explain, using precision/recall, why the high recall is now

   412→tool. Would you accept lower recall to get there? Justify it.
