     3→Chapter 14 — Delivery: CI/CD, DevOps, and Operations
     4→Where we are. Chapter 13 asked how AI changes the practice of software engineering; this chapter asks a
     5→question the earlier chapters quietly deferred. Chapters 1–12 taught you how to build software — process,
     6→requirements, design, patterns — and how to verify it with reviews, tests, security scanning, and metrics. But
     7→a verified commit sitting in a repository helps no one. This chapter covers the stretch of engineering between
     8→“the code is written” and “users are running it”; Chapter 15 covers the longer stretch after that — what
     9→happens to code once it has been in production for years. Delivery is where every earlier discipline either
    10→becomes real or stays theoretical.
    11→Delivery used to be an afterthought — a thing operations people did after engineering was “done.” Two shifts
    12→ended that. First, software moved to the cloud, where releasing stopped being a rare, ceremonial event and became
    13→something a team might do dozens of times a day. Second, a body of research (§14.7) showed that how a team
    14→delivers predicts its performance better than almost anything else it does. The umbrella term for the resulting
    15→culture is DevOps: the idea that developing software and operating it are one discipline, practiced by one team,
    16→with shared tools and shared accountability. This chapter walks the pipeline from commit to production, studies
    17→two of the most instructive deployment disasters on the public record, shows concretely how a service is packaged
    18→and put online — containers, a database, a domain, a certificate, and the edge — and closes with the research on
    19→why delivery performance predicts so much else. The fate that awaits all successful code — becoming legacy —
    20→is Chapter 15.
    21→Principle. Undeployed code is inventory, not value. Every practice in this chapter exists to shrink the time
    22→and the risk between writing a change and learning what it does in the hands of real users — the same short-
    23→feedback bet Chapter 2 made about process, now applied to the machinery of release itself.
    24→14.1 SaaS and the Cloud
    25→14.1.1 From Shipped Artifact to Running Service
    26→For most of software history, delivering software meant producing an artifact — a binary on tape, a shrink-
    27→wrapped CD, an installer download — that customers took away and ran on their machines. The vendor’s job
    28→ended at the factory gate. Under that model, releases are naturally rare and heavy: every artifact that leaves the
    29→building is effectively unpatchable for months, so it must be as close to perfect as testing can make it, and
    30→mistakes are answered with recalls, patch disks, and support calls.
    31→
    32→===== صفحة الكتاب 376 =====
    33→Software as a service (SaaS) inverts the arrangement: the software runs on machines the vendor controls, and
    34→users reach it over the network, usually through a browser or a thin client. In its pure form, nothing ships. That
    35→single change rearranges almost everything downstream. The provider controls what runs in production —
    36→canaries, staged rollouts, and feature flags may briefly mix versions, but there is no museum of old customer
    37→installs to support. The vendor can observe actual usage through logs, errors, and performance data, reducing its
    38→dependence on support reports. And because the vendor owns the machines, an update is an internal operation
    39→rather than a request to thousands of customers: it can happen at any time, invisibly, many times a day.
    40→Customers also benefit from the move toward SaaS: their data lives on the service, so a stolen laptop or a dead
    41→phone loses a device, not the work; several people can collaborate on the same data at once instead of emailing
    42→copies back and forth; and datasets that are large or constantly changing can live in one central, managed place
    43→rather than being squeezed onto whatever machine each user happens to own. For a whole class of applications —
    44→shared documents, shared calendars, anything with one truth that many people touch — the service model is both
    45→cheaper to operate and a better product.
    46→On the user’s side of the wire, the near-universal thin client is the browser: a program every device already has,
    47→speaking a front-end stack of HTML for the document’s structure, CSS for its presentation, and JavaScript for
    48→behavior that runs on the client — while the server side can be written in whatever language the team prefers,
    49→because the browser never sees it. Native mobile apps are the sibling front end, trading the browser’s universality
    50→for tighter platform integration; either way, the architecture is the same service behind a thin client.
    51→14.1.2 Multi-Tenancy and Horizontal Scaling
    52→Running the software yourself raises a design question: does each customer get their own copy? In a multi-tenant
    53→design, one running instance of the system serves many customers — tenants — whose data is kept logically
    54→separate inside shared infrastructure. Multi-tenancy is what makes SaaS economical: one fleet, one deployment,
    55→one upgrade for everyone, with the cost of operations amortized across all tenants. The price is that isolation
    56→becomes a software problem. A bug that leaks one tenant’s data to another, or one tenant’s heavy load that starves
    57→the rest (the “noisy neighbor”), are failure modes a shipped artifact simply could not have.
    58→Serving many tenants also means serving unpredictable load, and the cloud’s answer is horizontal scaling:
    59→instead of buying a bigger machine (vertical scaling), you run more copies of the same service behind a load
    60→balancer and add or remove copies as demand moves. Horizontal scaling only works cleanly if any copy can serve
    61→any request — which is exactly why the statelessness convention of RESTful design matters (§7.5.4). A service
    62→that keeps per-client session state on one server has silently welded each user to that server; a stateless one lets the
    63→platform treat servers as interchangeable, replaceable cattle. Architecture decisions from Chapter 7 are, it turns
    64→out, delivery decisions too.

    67→14.1.3 What the Cloud Does to the Iron Triangle
    68→Recall the iron triangle of Chapter 1 (§1.5): scope, schedule, and cost constrain each other, and the classic pressure
    69→point was the release date — a single, high-stakes deadline toward which scope was cut and quality was crunched.
    70→SaaS dissolves that pressure point. When deploying is cheap and continuous, release stops being an event and
    71→becomes a decision — a small, revocable, per-feature decision made many times a week. You no longer ask
    72→“what can we finish by the ship date?” but “is this one change ready to meet users now?” Schedule pressure does
    73→not vanish; it decomposes into many tiny pressures, each too small to justify heroics.
    74→That is the economic root of the deploy-early culture. Teams that deploy each small change as it is finished get
    75→feedback while the change is still fresh in their heads, keep the gap between “works on my machine” and “works
    76→in production” permanently small, and never build up a terrifying pile of unreleased work whose first contact with
    77→reality is a big bang — the same pathology, at the release level, that §2.4.1 diagnosed at the integration level. The
    78→practices in the remainder of this chapter reduce the risk of early deployment.
    79→14.1.4 The Cloud Landscape: Providers, Components, and Responsibility
    80→The vendor-controlled machines of §14.1.1 have to live somewhere, and today they mostly live in a public cloud:
    81→vast provider-owned datacenters whose capacity is rented out over the network, by the hour or by the request. As
    82→of 2025, the market is led by Amazon Web Services (AWS), Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure, with a long tail
    83→of smaller providers — DigitalOcean, Linode, Hetzner — that trade breadth of catalog for simplicity and price.1
    84→Hold the names loosely: provider names and product lists date quickly, but the concepts beneath them do not.
    85→Every cloud, whatever its branding, sells the same four component families. Compute is processing capacity —
    86→virtual machines, containers, or functions that run your code. Storage is durable data — disks, object stores,
    87→managed databases. Network is the plumbing — private networks, load balancers, DNS — that lets those pieces
    88→reach each other and the world. And security and identity is the control plane over the other three: who, and
    89→which programs, may do what to which resources.
    90→The cloud’s economic promise is threefold. You pay only for what you use, turning a large up-front hardware
    91→purchase into a metered utility bill. You can place your system in regions around the world, near your users,
    92→without signing a lease on another continent. And the hardware is someone else’s problem — provisioning, failed
    93→disks, power, cooling, and physical security are all handled below the waterline of the service you rent.
    94→What the provider explicitly does not handle is captured by the shared responsibility model: the provider secures
    95→the cloud — the facilities, the hardware, the hypervisor — while you secure what is in it — your operating-system
    96→configuration, your code, your data, and who can access all three.2 This is a legal line as much as a practical one,
    97→and teams forget it at their peril: a world-readable storage bucket full of customer records is your breach, however
    98→secure the datacenter around it was.
    99→None of this makes the cloud automatic. The alternatives — on-premises servers you own, or rented space and
   100→machines in a shared datacenter (co-location) — never went away, and the honest question is when each wins.
   101→
   102→===== صفحة الكتاب 378 =====
   103→Cloud makes clear sense when you do not yet know what hardware you need, when demand is spiky or
   104→unpredictable (provision for the spike and pay for it forever, or scale for the hour it lasts), when speed to a first
   105→MVP matters more than unit cost, and when a small team has no one to spare for operations.
   106→Case study. Cloud repatriation. The trade cuts the other way, too. In 2022–23, 37signals — the company
   107→behind Basecamp — publicly moved its products off the cloud and onto purchased hardware, documenting
   108→seven-figure annual savings for workloads that were steady and predictable rather than spiky.3 4 And
   109→Amazon’s own Prime Video team published an account of cutting the cost of one audio/video monitoring
   110→service by roughly 90 percent — by moving it away from a serverless, distributed-microservices design and
   111→back into a monolith-style process.5 None of this means the cloud is over. The cloud is a cost-and-flexibility
   112→trade, not an axiom. Elastic, uncertain load is where renting wins; steady, predictable load is where owning
   113→wins. Run the numbers, not the fashion.
   114→14.1.5 Containers, Clusters, and Kubernetes
   115→The unit of cloud deployment that has won in practice is the container: a package containing an application plus
   116→everything it needs to run — language runtime, libraries, configuration — isolated from other software on the
   117→same machine while sharing the host operating system’s kernel. Sharing the kernel is what distinguishes a
   118→container from a virtual machine, which carries an entire guest operating system of its own: a container starts in
   119→seconds rather than minutes, and a single host can run dozens of them.6 The container image is the immutable,
   120→versioned artifact that a CI pipeline (§14.2.2) builds exactly once and then deploys everywhere — the same byte-
   121→for-byte thing on a laptop, in the test environment, and in production, which retires “works on my machine” as a
   122→category of excuse.
   123→One host is rarely enough, so containers are run on a cluster: a set of network-connected machines managed as a
   124→single pool of compute, storage, and memory. And the de-facto standard software for managing that pool is
   125→Kubernetes (84% of organizations in CNCF’s 2023 survey were using or evaluating it), often described as the
   126→operating system for the cloud: just as an OS schedules processes onto CPU cores, Kubernetes schedules
   127→containers onto the machines of a cluster.7 Around that core job it handles ingress (routing incoming traffic to the
   128→right containers), scaling the number of running copies up and down with demand, restarting containers that crash
   129→or fail health checks, and attaching storage to containers that need it — the operational chores of §14.1.2’s
   130→horizontal scaling, automated.
   131→One caveat: Kubernetes earns its considerable complexity at fleet scale. For a small team — certainly for a class
   132→project — a single container on one host, or a platform-as-a-service that runs your container for you, delivers most
   133→of the benefit at a small fraction of the operational cost. Learn what Kubernetes is for; reach for it when you have
   134→the problem it solves.

   137→14.1.6 Distributed Trade-Offs: The CAP Theorem
   138→Horizontal scaling (§14.1.2) eventually reaches the data layer, and the moment state is replicated — living on
   139→more than one machine — a hard theoretical limit applies. Eric Brewer’s CAP theorem concerns three properties
   140→of a distributed data system: consistency (every read sees the most recent write), availability (every request
   141→receives a response), and partition tolerance (the system keeps operating when the network splits and some
   142→machines cannot reach others).8 Its real content is narrower than the popular “pick two of three”: in the presence
   143→of a partition, a replicated system cannot guarantee both strong consistency and availability, so for that data it
   144→must choose which of the two to weaken until the network heals.9
   145→Since partitions are a fact of real networks — switches fail, cables get cut, datacenters lose connectivity —
   146→partition tolerance is not really optional, and the practical content of the theorem is a forced choice during a
   147→partition: consistency or availability. A replica that cannot reach the others can either refuse to answer (staying
   148→consistent but unavailable) or answer from possibly stale data (staying available but inconsistent). Which to
   149→sacrifice is a requirements question, not a technical one. A bank chooses consistency: better to refuse service for a
   150→minute than to show two customers different balances for the same account. A social feed chooses availability: a
   151→like count that is a few seconds stale harms no one, but an unreachable feed is a product failure.
   152→System

   161→The theorem is why architecture and delivery keep meeting in this chapter: the shared-data pattern (§7.2.1) put
   162→one authoritative store at the center of a system, and scaling that store horizontally (§14.1.2) replicates it — at
   163→which point CAP stops being theory and becomes a decision your team must make on purpose, per feature, with
   164→the requirements in hand.
   165→14.2 Continuous Integration Pipelines
   166→Chapter 2 introduced continuous integration (CI) as an XP engineering practice: everyone merges into a shared
   167→mainline many times a day, and an automated build-and-test run verifies each merge (§2.3.4). That definition told
   168→you what CI is and why it exists — to surface integration mismatches one small change at a time instead of all at
   169→once. This section goes deeper: what the automation actually consists of, what branching discipline it demands,
   170→and what social contract makes it work.
   171→14.2.1 Trunk-Based Development
   172→CI is first a branching policy, and only second a server. In trunk-based development, the whole team commits to
   173→a single shared mainline (the trunk), directly or through short-lived branches that live hours to a couple of days
   174→
   175→===== صفحة الكتاب 380 =====
   176→before merging. The alternative — long-lived feature branches, where each developer or feature camps on its
   177→own branch for weeks — feels safer because nobody’s half-done work disturbs anyone else. But the safety is an
   178→illusion with interest due at merge time: the longer two branches evolve apart, the more their eventual merge
   179→resembles the big-bang integration of §2.4.1, with conflicting assumptions surfacing all at once, weeks after they
   180→were made.
   181→Integration becomes more difficult as branches diverge, and divergence increases with time. Trunk-based
   182→development keeps divergence permanently small by construction. Merging several times a day means each merge
   183→carries at most a few hours of divergence — small enough that conflicts are rare, and trivial when they occur. The
   184→obvious objection — “how do I commit work that isn’t finished?” — has a standard answer you will meet in
   185→§14.3.3: hide unfinished work behind a feature flag so it can be integrated without being released. Integration
   186→and release become independent decisions, which is one of the most useful separations in this whole chapter.
   187→14.2.2 The Stages of a Pipeline
   188→A CI pipeline is the automated gauntlet every commit runs before it is declared good. The pipeline is where
   189→earlier chapters’ verification techniques stop being activities a diligent person might perform and become gates no
   190→change can skip. A typical pipeline runs stages in increasing order of cost, failing fast on the cheap ones:

   193→Take the stages in order. The build proves the change even compiles and its dependencies resolve — the cheapest
   194→possible check, so it runs first. Static checks run the automated analysis of Chapter 9 (§9.4) — linters, type
   195→checkers, style and bug-pattern analyzers — catching whole classes of defects without executing a line. Then the
   196→tests by level from Chapter 10 run in pyramid order: unit tests first because they are fast and localize failures
   197→precisely, then integration, then a thin layer of end-to-end tests. If everything passes, the pipeline produces a
   198→versioned artifact — a container image, a package, a binary — that is stored and never rebuilt. This last rule
   199→matters more than it looks: the artifact you tested is byte-for-byte the artifact you will deploy. Rebuilding “the
   200→same code” later invites the possibility that a changed compiler, dependency, or build machine produces
   201→something subtly different from what passed the tests. Mature pipelines add one more gate on the far side of
   202→deployment itself: a smoke-test stage — a fast is-it-alive check (does the service start, answer a trivial request,

   205→reach its database?) run against the newly deployed version, gating the rollout before real traffic widens onto it

   207→14.2.3 Broken-Build Discipline
   208→A pipeline is only as good as the team’s response when it turns red. The working culture of CI rests on a small
   209→social contract. First, a red mainline is everyone’s emergency: when the trunk build breaks, fixing it (or
   210→reverting the breaking commit) takes priority over new work, because every hour the build stays red, every other
   211→developer is either blocked or building on sand. Second, never commit onto a broken build — you would be
   212→stacking unverified change on unverified change, exactly the divergence CI exists to prevent. Third, do not leave
   213→a broken build overnight; revert if you cannot fix quickly. Reverting is not an insult. It is the cheap, always-
   214→available path back to a known-good state, and teams that treat it as routine stay green far more than teams that
   215→treat it as defeat.
   216→A broken build interrupts the entire team’s work and should be treated as an urgent operational problem. While it
   217→is green, every developer has a continuous, machine-checked guarantee that the shared codebase works and can
   218→build on top of it; while it is red, nobody knows whether the system works, and restoring it comes before other
   219→work.
   220→Pitfall. The flaky test. A test that fails intermittently without a code change is more corrosive than a test that
   221→always fails, because it teaches the team to ignore red. Once “just re-run it, that one’s flaky” enters the
   222→vocabulary, the build has become a slot machine, and real failures start slipping through on the same shrug.
   223→Quarantine flaky tests immediately, fix or delete them promptly, and treat their existence as a defect in the
   224→suite. §10.2.4 catalogs the usual causes — races, shared state, order dependence, unstable externals — and
   225→their fixes.
   226→14.2.4 Keeping Pipelines Fast
   227→Pipeline speed is a hard constraint, not a convenience. Developers are supposed to merge several times a day and
   228→to wait for green before moving on. If the pipeline takes an hour, they will not wait — they will batch up bigger
   229→changes to amortize the wait, which re-creates the large, risky merges CI exists to eliminate. A useful target is
   230→roughly ten minutes from push to verdict for the merge-blocking stages.10
   231→Achieving that is the testing pyramid of §10.2.4 applied as an engineering budget: push checks down the pyramid,
   232→where they are fast, and keep the slow end-to-end layer thin. Beyond that, run independent stages in parallel,
   233→cache dependencies and build outputs so unchanged parts are not rebuilt, and split the pipeline into a fast merge-
   234→blocking core plus deeper suites (full end-to-end runs, performance tests, long fuzzing) that run continuously
   235→against the trunk without holding up merges.
   236→
   237→===== صفحة الكتاب 383 =====
   238→Order matters inside the merge-blocking core, too. The pipeline’s verdict arrives when the first test fails, not when
   239→the last one passes — so run the tests most likely to fail first: the ones nearest the changed code, and the ones that
   240→have failed most recently. At the largest scale, teams select per change the subset of tests worth running at all — a
   241→budgeted-selection problem (the knapsack problem in disguise) that §10.1.6 examines, and that industrial CI
   242→systems implement as predictive test selection. Treat a slow pipeline as a process defect that changes how your
   243→team behaves, not as a tooling annoyance to live with.
   244→14.3 Continuous Deployment
   245→14.3.1 Delivery versus Deployment
   246→Two similar terms name genuinely different commitments. Continuous delivery means every change that passes
   247→the pipeline yields an artifact that is proven deployable — the release decision is a business choice, but it is always
   248→available, at the push of a button, with no additional engineering work. Continuous deployment goes one step
   249→further: every change that passes the pipeline is deployed to production automatically, with no human in the loop.
   250→Delivery makes release possible at any moment; deployment makes it actual at every moment.
   251→Continuous deployment sounds reckless until you notice what it forbids. If every green commit goes to
   252→production, then there is no such thing as a “safe to merge but not ready to ship” change without a flag, no manual
   253→pre-release checklist to lean on, and no batching of changes into a big release whose failures cannot be attributed.
   254→Every safety property must be automated, because automation is all there is. Teams that adopt it report a
   255→paradoxical result that §14.7 will make precise: deploying more often makes each deployment less risky, because
   256→each one is smaller, better attributed, and easier to undo.
   257→14.3.2 Deployment Strategies
   258→However often you deploy, how you swap new code into a live system determines the blast radius when
   259→something is wrong. Two infrastructure-level strategies dominate practice; a third approach moves the switch into
   260→code, and it gets its own section (§14.3.3).
   261→Blue-green deployment runs two identical production environments. At any moment one (say, blue) serves all
   262→traffic while the other (green) sits idle. To release, you deploy the new version to the idle environment, verify it
   263→there against production conditions, then switch the router so all traffic flows to it. The old environment stays
   264→warm, so if the new version misbehaves, recovery is one router change back. You pay for this in doubled
   265→infrastructure and in the care demanded by anything stateful: a database schema shared by both environments
   266→must remain compatible with both versions during the switch.
   267→Canary deployment releases the new version to a small slice of traffic first — one server, one percent of users,
   268→one region — and watches error rates, latency, and business metrics before widening. The name comes from the
   269→caged canary that warned miners of gas: the small exposed population absorbs the harm and sounds the alarm
   270→
   271→===== صفحة الكتاب 384 =====
   272→while the damage is still bounded. Large operators generalize canaries into staged rings: the change rolls to ring
   273→after ring — internal users, then a small public slice, then broader populations — with automated health checks
   274→gating each promotion and halting the rollout on regression. The essential idea is progressive exposure: no change
   275→reaches everyone until it has demonstrably survived contact with someone.
   276→14.3.3 Feature Flags: Decoupling Deploy from Release
   277→Blue-green and canary deployments control exposure with infrastructure — routers, server pools, traffic slices. A
   278→feature flag (or feature toggle) moves the same control into code: a runtime conditional that turns a code path on
   279→or off without redeploying.11 Deploying and releasing become fully independent acts. A feature’s code can sit in
   280→production for weeks, dark and disabled, while the team keeps merging; when the moment comes, “releasing” it is
   281→a configuration change that takes effect in seconds, and un-releasing it is the same change in reverse. This is the
   282→endpoint of §14.1.3’s argument: release is no longer even a deployment decision — it is a bit you flip.
   283→Flags are also the standard answer to §14.2.1’s objection (“how do I commit work that isn’t finished?”):
   284→unfinished work merges to trunk dark and disabled, so integration stays continuous while release waits. In practice
   285→flags come in a few kinds with very different lifespans, and confusing them is where trouble starts:
   286→Release flags hide work in progress until it is ready. They should live days to weeks — a release flag whose
   287→feature has fully rolled out is a deletion you owe.
   288→Operational flags — including the kill switch — guard risky paths so an operator can disable a
   289→misbehaving feature instantly, without a deploy. These are deliberately long-lived, few in number, and
   290→tested like the safety equipment they are.
   291→Experiment flags split traffic between variants so you can measure a change (the A/B tests your metrics
   292→chapter made honest — §12.5). They live exactly as long as the experiment.
   293→Targeting is what makes flags more than an on/off switch: a flag can be on for one user, one tenant, or three
   294→percent of traffic — a percentage rollout, which is canary deployment’s progressive-exposure idea (§14.3.2)
   295→implemented in code, with no second environment to pay for.

   335→14.3.4 Rollback versus Roll-Forward
   336→When a deployment goes wrong, you have two exits. Rollback returns production to the previous version; roll-
   337→forward ships a new fix on top of the broken state. Rollback is usually faster and requires no new (unverified)
   338→code, so mature teams treat it as the default reflex. But a rollback path is a mechanism, and Chapter 10’s lesson
   339→applies to mechanisms too: an untested rollback is a rumor, not a capability. Version skew can make the old code
   340→unable to read data the new code wrote; a config change may have accompanied the code; the “previous artifact”
   341→may no longer exist. Teams that take this seriously rehearse rollback routinely — some by making it the normal
   342→end of every canary that fails a health check, so the path is exercised weekly rather than discovered during a crisis.
   343→And some changes cannot be rolled back at all (an irreversible data migration, a security fix you must not un-
   344→ship), which is why roll-forward speed — how fast your pipeline can carry a one-line fix to production — is itself
   345→a safety property.
   346→14.3.5 When Deployment Goes Wrong: Two Case Studies
   347→The two case studies below are, respectively, the strongest argument on record for deployment automation and the
   348→strongest argument that automation alone is not safety. They are worth studying closely, and honestly, from the
   349→primary sources.
   350→
   351→===== صفحة الكتاب 387 =====
   352→Case study. Knight Capital, August 1, 2012. Knight Capital was one of the largest equity market makers in
   353→the United States. When the New York Stock Exchange launched its Retail Liquidity Program, Knight
   354→updated SMARS — its automated order router — to participate. What follows is drawn from the findings in
   355→the SEC’s later enforcement order (Release No. 34-70694, October 2013).17
   356→The new code reused a feature flag that had previously activated “Power Peg,” defunct order-routing
   357→functionality unused since 2003, whose dead code had been left in SMARS. Worse, the safety counter that
   358→once told Power Peg to stop when orders were filled had been inadvertently disabled in 2005, when the
   359→counter was moved elsewhere in the code and Power Peg was never retested. In the week before launch, a
   360→technician manually copied the new code onto SMARS’s eight production servers — and missed one. No
   361→second person reviewed the deployment; there was no automated, repeatable deployment process to make
   362→the eight servers provably identical.
   363→On the morning of August 1, orders routed to the un-updated eighth server hit the repurposed flag and woke
   364→the old Power Peg code, which began generating child orders continuously, never tracking fills, never
   365→stopping. Ninety-seven automated email messages referencing the Power Peg error had gone out before the
   366→market opened; no one acted on them — they were not designed as alerts, and they went to a group of
   367→personnel rather than to an owner with a duty to respond. In the roughly forty-five minutes that followed,
   368→Knight executed about four million trades across 154 stocks — on the order of 397 million shares — and,
   369→during diagnosis, engineers made the situation worse: suspecting the new code, they rolled it back on the
   370→seven correct servers, which put the repurposed flag’s old behavior in force on all eight. There was no
   371→procedure for halting the system’s aberrant activity and no documented incident-response plan. The loss
   372→exceeded $460 million. Knight survived only through an emergency investment18 and was merged away
   373→within a year; the SEC fined it $12 million for violating market-access risk-control and related rules.
   374→A note on honesty: the SEC order is an enforcement action about risk controls, and the deployment details
   375→above are findings within it — the record shows a chain of process failures, not the folklore version in which
   376→“one line of config” destroyed a company. The lessons are about the chain. Deployment must be automated,
   377→repeatable, and verified — an all-servers-identical check would have caught the eighth server in seconds.
   378→Never repurpose an old flag; delete dead code rather than leaving it armed. Alerts without owners are noise.
   379→Rollback is only a safety mechanism if it has been tested as one — here it was the step that completed the
   380→disaster. And a kill switch is required, not optional.
   381→
   382→===== صفحة الكتاب 388 =====
   383→Case study. CrowdStrike, July 19, 2024. CrowdStrike’s Falcon sensor is endpoint security software that runs
   384→inside the Windows kernel — the most privileged, least forgiving place code can live. To respond to new
   385→threats quickly, CrowdStrike ships “Rapid Response Content”: threat-detection configuration delivered to all
   386→customers through a fully automated global push. The account below follows CrowdStrike’s own external
   387→root-cause analysis, published in August 2024.19
   388→In February 2024, sensor version 7.11 added a new content type defined with twenty-one input fields. The
   389→code that supplied those inputs provided twenty. The mismatch stayed latent for months because both the
   390→tests and all earlier content of that type used a wildcard match for the twenty-first field, so the missing input
   391→was never read. On July 19, a new content instance — Channel File 291 — used a non-wildcard twenty-first
   392→field for the first time. Reading the field that was not there caused an out-of-bounds memory read inside the
   393→kernel, crashing Windows. Because the sensor loads at boot, the machine crashed again on restart: a boot
   394→loop. The automated Content Validator that should have rejected the bad content had a bug of its own and
   395→passed it.
   396→The push was global and simultaneous. Within hours, roughly 8.5 million Windows machines (Microsoft’s
   397→estimate) were down: airlines (Delta alone canceled on the order of 7,000 flights), hospitals, banks,
   398→broadcasters, emergency services.20 21 Damage estimates ran into the billions — direct losses for the
   399→Fortune 500 alone were estimated at $5.4 billion, only a fraction of it insured.22 Recovery was brutal
   400→precisely because the machines could not boot: in many cases a human had to start each machine in safe
   401→mode and delete the file by hand.23 CrowdStrike’s committed remediations read like this chapter’s outline:
   402→staged canary rings for content, customer control over update cadence, a hardened validator, bounds
   403→checking in the interpreter, and more diverse testing.
   404→The lessons generalize far beyond security software. Config and content are code: anything that changes
   405→the behavior of a running system deserves the same testing, staging, and progressive rollout as a code
   406→change, no matter how routine its format. A fully automated pipeline without progressive exposure is not
   407→safety — it is a machine for shipping a defect to every user on Earth at once. Validators are code too, with
   408→false negatives of their own (§9.4.2); a gate you never test is a gate you cannot trust. Design for a bounded
   409→blast radius before you need one. And recovery paths must be designed for the worst case — a fix pushed
   410→over the network is useless to a machine that cannot boot to receive it.
   411→Read as a pair, the two cases bracket this chapter’s argument. Knight shows what manual deployment costs:
   412→without automation, you cannot even guarantee that eight servers run the same code. CrowdStrike shows what
   413→automation without staging costs: with a perfect distribution machine and no progressive rollout, one latent defect
   414→reached the whole world before anyone could react. Twelve years apart, the timelines rhyme — about forty-five
   415→minutes for Knight’s loss, about eighty minutes from CrowdStrike’s push to its reversion.24 Automation sets the
   416→speed of your outcomes; only progressive exposure and tested recovery decide their sign.

   502→14.4.3 Stateful Services: Postgres and Redis
   503→Two of those services deserve a closer look, because they hold state, and state is where deployment gets hard. A
   504→container is ephemeral: stop it and everything written inside its writable layer is gone. That is exactly what you
   505→want for the stateless app container, which you replace wholesale on every deploy, and exactly what you must
   506→prevent for a database. The fix is a volume: storage that lives outside any container’s lifecycle and is mounted into
   507→one. In the file above, db-data:/var/lib/postgresql/data mounts a Docker-managed volume at the directory
   508→where Postgres keeps its files, so the data survives docker compose down and the next deploy.
   509→The two data services play different roles, and the difference is a direct instance of the CAP trade-off (§14.1.6)
   510→and the shared-data pattern (§7.2.1):
   511→PostgreSQL is the system of record: a relational database offering transactions, constraints, and durability.
   512→When correctness matters — a patient record, a payment, an appointment — the data goes here, and
   513→Postgres guarantees a committed write is not lost. It is the authoritative store §7.2.1 told you to design
   514→around.
   515→Redis is an in-memory cache and data-structure store: it keeps data in RAM, so reads and writes are
   516→very fast, and it holds work that tolerates loss — session tokens, rate-limit counters, computed results you
   517→can recompute, a queue of background jobs. Putting a cache in front of Postgres is the usual way to make a
   518→read-heavy service fast, and it is safe precisely because the cache is disposable: if Redis loses its data, the
   519→system reads from Postgres and refills it.
   520→Pitfall. Treating the cache as a source of truth. The moment your system cannot rebuild Redis’s contents
   521→from the database, you have quietly made an in-memory store your system of record, and Redis by default
   522→has promised you no such durability. Keep the authoritative copy in the durable store and let the cache stay
   523→something you can throw away and rebuild at any time.
   524→Both Postgres and Redis ship as official images on the public registry, maintained and security-patched by the
   525→wider community, which is why the Compose file pulls postgres:16 and redis:7 instead of installing and
   526→configuring database servers by hand.27 Pin the major version, as with any dependency (§14.6.2), so an
   527→unattended pull cannot upgrade your database engine underneath you.
   528→14.4.4 Configuration and Secrets
   529→The Compose file contains neither a password embedded in the image nor a connection string hard-coded in the
   530→source. Those values arrive as environment variables (${DB_PASSWORD}), read from an untracked .env file or
   531→injected by the deployment platform. This is the externalized-configuration rule of the twelve-factor model: one
   532→image runs in every environment, and what differs between environments — credentials, hostnames, feature
   533→toggles — lives in configuration outside the image.28
   534→
   535→===== صفحة الكتاب 393 =====
   536→The rule earns its keep twice. It keeps a single image promotable from test to production without a rebuild, and it
   537→keeps secrets out of the image and out of version control, where §14.6.3 shows they cause real breaches once
   538→leaked. A committed .env file is one of the most common ways a live credential ends up in a public repository.
   539→Principle. One image, many environments. If moving a build from staging to production requires rebuilding
   540→it, the build was never really the artifact; its configuration was. Externalize what changes between
   541→environments so the thing you tested is exactly the thing you ship.
   542→14.5 Reaching Users: DNS, TLS, and the Edge
   543→You now have a stack running on a server. That server has an IP address like 203.0.113.143, but no user will type
   544→that, and no browser will trust it without a padlock. Two pieces of internet infrastructure bridge the distance
   545→between “a container is running somewhere” and “anyone can reach it safely at https://clinic.example.com”:
   546→the Domain Name System, which turns a name into an address, and TLS, which encrypts and authenticates the
   547→connection. This section explains both, adds the layer most real deployments put in front of everything else, and
   548→closes with a complete deployment from start to finish.
   549→14.5.1 How a Name Becomes an Address
   550→The Domain Name System (DNS) is the internet’s directory: a globally distributed database that maps human-
   551→readable names like clinic.example.com to the numeric IP addresses machines actually route to.29 A client’s first
   552→connection to an uncached hostname generally requires name resolution — commonly through DNS — and
   553→understanding the pieces clears up what is otherwise the most common source of “it works locally but not in
   554→production.”
   555→You obtain a domain from a registrar, the company you buy example.com from. The registrar records which
   556→nameservers are authoritative for your domain — the servers that hold the real answers. When someone visits
   557→your site, their computer asks a resolver (usually run by their ISP, or a public one like 1.1.1.1), which walks the
   558→hierarchy: it asks a root server who handles .com, asks that TLD server who is authoritative for example.com, and
   559→finally asks your authoritative nameserver for the specific record. Each answer is cached along the way for a
   560→period set by the record’s TTL (time to live), which is why a DNS change is not instant: older cached answers
   561→linger until their TTL expires. The authoritative nameserver holds records, each a typed mapping. The handful
   562→you actually set for a deployment:

   589→14.5.2 HTTPS and TLS Certificates
   590→A DNS lookup gets a browser to your server; TLS (Transport Layer Security, the protocol behind the s in https)
   591→makes the connection between them private and trustworthy. It does two things at once: it encrypts the traffic so
   592→no one on the network path can read or alter it, and it authenticates the server so the browser knows it is really
   593→talking to clinic.example.com and not an impostor. Modern browsers now treat plain HTTP as a defect, labeling
   594→it “Not Secure,” and the large majority of page loads happen over HTTPS.30 For a real deployment, TLS is not a
   595→nicety to add later.
   596→Authentication rests on a certificate: a file, issued by a Certificate Authority (CA), that binds your domain name
   597→to a cryptographic key and carries the signature of an authority the browser already trusts. When a browser
   598→connects, the server presents its certificate; the browser checks the CA’s signature and that the name matches, then
   599→negotiates an encrypted session. A certificate for a name you do not control cannot normally be obtained from a
   600→trusted CA, which is what stops an attacker from impersonating your site.
   601→Certificates were once a paid, manual chore. Let’s Encrypt changed that: a nonprofit CA that issues certificates
   602→for free and fully automatically over the ACME protocol, which lets a program prove it controls a domain and
   603→receive a certificate with no human in the loop.31 Let’s Encrypt now secures over 700 million sites and issues on
   604→the order of ten million certificates a day.32 Its certificates are deliberately short-lived and auto-renewed: a
   605→program renews them well before they expire, so a forgotten manual renewal can no longer take a site down. In
   606→practice you rarely touch a certificate directly — a reverse proxy such as nginx or Caddy sits in front of your app
   607→

   609→container, obtains and renews the certificate, terminates TLS, and forwards plain HTTP to the app over the private
   610→network.
   611→14.5.3 The Edge: CDNs and Cloudflare
   612→That reverse-proxy idea generalizes into one of the most important pieces of modern deployment: a layer at the
   613→edge, between your users and your server, that handles concerns you would rather not build yourself. The
   614→dominant provider is Cloudflare, and the mechanism is a small DNS change. Instead of pointing your domain
   615→straight at your server, you point it at Cloudflare, which proxies each request to your origin server behind the
   616→scenes.33 Your server’s real address is hidden, and every request flows through Cloudflare first. Sitting in that
   617→position lets the edge do several jobs:
   618→Content delivery (CDN). Cloudflare runs servers in hundreds of cities and caches your static content close
   619→to users, so a visitor in Sydney is served from Sydney rather than waiting on a round trip to your origin.
   620→This cuts latency and offloads your server.
   621→TLS termination. The edge presents the HTTPS certificate and encrypts the user connection, so you get
   622→valid TLS without configuring it on the origin at all.
   623→Security. Because all traffic passes through it, the edge can absorb distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)
   624→attacks — floods of traffic meant to exhaust your server — and filter malicious requests with a web
   625→application firewall (WAF) before they reach you.
   626→The scale is what makes this remarkable. Cloudflare sits in front of roughly one in five of all websites, so a large
   627→share of the web is proxied through this one network.34 That scale is the source of both its value, because it sees
   628→enough traffic to recognize new attacks quickly, and a real concern: when a service this central has an outage, a
   629→visible slice of the web goes down with it. The concentration is worth weighing when you decide how much of
   630→your delivery to place behind a single provider.
   631→Pitfall. Assuming the edge makes your origin safe to ignore. Cloudflare hides and protects your origin only
   632→if the origin is not also reachable at its real IP address. If an attacker finds the origin IP and your server still
   633→accepts direct connections, they bypass the edge entirely. Configure the origin to accept traffic only from the
   634→edge, so the protection cannot be stepped around.

   665→14.6 Continuous Security Pipelines
   666→Chapter 9 taught static analysis as a practice; the pipeline is where it becomes policy. Modern teams extend the CI
   667→pipeline of §14.2 into a continuous security pipeline — a set of automated gates that check not just whether the
   668→code works, but whether it is safe to expose to an adversarial world. Three scanner families divide the work.
   669→14.6.1 SAST, DAST, and SCA
   670→Static application security testing (SAST) is the security-focused end of the static analysis you met in §9.4:
   671→tools that examine source code without running it, hunting injection flaws, unsafe deserialization, buffer misuse,
   672→and other vulnerable patterns. Everything Chapter 9 said about false positives and false negatives applies with
   673→interest — a noisy SAST gate that developers learn to rubber-stamp protects no one.
   674→Dynamic application security testing (DAST) attacks the running application from outside, the way an
   675→adversary would: probing endpoints with malformed inputs, injection payloads, and authentication bypasses,
   676→knowing nothing about the source. SAST and DAST are complementary the way white-box and black-box testing
   677→were in Chapter 10: SAST sees code paths DAST may never reach; DAST sees emergent, deployed behavior —
   678→server configuration, header mistakes, the composition of services — that no source scan can.35
   679→Software composition analysis (SCA) examines neither your code nor your running app but your dependency
   680→manifest: the inventory of third-party packages your build pulls in, checked against databases of known
   681→vulnerabilities. SCA exists because of an uncomfortable arithmetic: in a typical modern application, code you
   682→wrote is a thin layer atop orders of magnitude more code you imported. You ship your dependencies. Their
   683→vulnerabilities are your vulnerabilities, and no review of your code will find them.
   684→14.6.2 Dependencies and the Supply Chain
   685→Because dependencies drift out of date on their own — vulnerabilities are discovered in versions you already ship
   686→— SCA cannot be a one-time gate; it must run continuously. The practical pattern is the automated update bot
   687→(GitHub’s Dependabot is the archetype): a service that watches vulnerability databases and your manifests, and
   688→when a dependency needs bumping, opens a pull request that updates it.36 The elegance is in what happens next:
   689→your CI pipeline runs on that PR like any other, so the same suite that protects you from your own mistakes now
   690→proves the upgrade is safe to merge. The stronger your pipeline, the cheaper staying current becomes — one more
   691→return on the investment of §14.2.
   692→The wider issue is supply-chain risk: your build is only as trustworthy as everything it downloads. Attackers have
   693→learned to poison the well — typosquatting packages whose names are one keystroke from a popular library, or
   694→compromising a legitimate package’s maintainer account and publishing a malicious release. The 2020
   695→SolarWinds attack planted malicious code inside a vendor’s build process, so customers received a compromised
   696→product signed with authentic signatures;37 the 2016 left-pad incident showed the fragility side, when the removal
   697→of an eleven-line package briefly broke builds across the industry.38 39 Defenses are accumulating — lockfiles
   698→
   699→===== صفحة الكتاب 398 =====
   700→that pin exact versions, cryptographic signing and provenance attestation for artifacts (the SLSA framework),40
   701→and a software bill of materials (SBOM) enumerating everything inside a release41 — but the first defense is the
   702→cultural one: treat adding a dependency as an engineering decision with a threat model, not a free lunch. Chapter
   703→11 develops this into a full treatment of supply-chain security — the Log4Shell and xz-utils case studies, and a
   704→framework for continuously verifying the open-source components you depend on.
   705→14.6.3 Secrets and Gate Placement
   706→One more scanner belongs in every pipeline: secrets scanning, which searches commits for credentials — API
   707→keys, tokens, passwords, private keys — before they enter history. A secret pushed to a repository must be treated
   708→as compromised the moment it lands, because git history is effectively permanent and harvesting bots scan public
   709→commits continuously. Rotating a leaked credential is painful; a pre-commit or pre-receive scan that blocks the
   710→leak is nearly free.
   711→Placement follows one principle: run each gate at the earliest point it can give a correct answer. Secrets scans
   712→and SAST need only source, so they run at commit time, inside the fast merge-blocking core. SCA needs the
   713→resolved dependency set, so it runs at build time — and again on a schedule, since the world’s knowledge of your
   714→dependencies changes while your code does not. DAST needs a running system, so it runs against a staging
   715→deployment, after the artifact exists. The result is defense in depth through the pipeline itself: by the time an
   716→artifact reaches production, it has been examined as source, as a composition, and as a running target.
   717→14.7 DORA Delivery Metrics
   718→14.7.1 The Four Keys — Now Five
   719→How would you know whether any of this is working? As Chapter 12 explained, metrics programs often choose
   720→convenient measurements that do not reflect the outcomes the team needs to improve. The delivery world has an
   721→unusually good answer, produced by the DORA research program (DevOps Research and Assessment) — a multi-
   722→year academic effort, surveying tens of thousands of professionals, published in the annual State of DevOps
   723→reports and the book Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble, and Kim).42 43 Its best-known finding is a set of outcome
   724→measures — the famous four keys — that jointly predict software-delivery performance:
   725→1. Deployment frequency — how often your team deploys to production.
   726→2. Change lead time — how long a commit takes to reach production.
   727→3. Change fail rate — what fraction of deployments cause a failure in production (an incident, a rollback, a
   728→hotfix).
   729→4. Failed-deployment recovery time — when a deployment does cause a failure, how long restoring service
   730→takes.44
   731→
   732→===== صفحة الكتاب 399 =====
   733→The model has since been refined: the 2024 research added a fifth metric, deployment rework rate — the share
   734→of deployments that are unplanned, shipped in response to a production incident — and DORA’s current guidance
   735→presents the five in two groups.45 Throughput is change lead time, deployment frequency, and failed-deployment
   736→recovery time: how fast changes — including fixes — flow to production. Instability is change fail rate and
   737→deployment rework rate: how often deployments go wrong, or exist only to clean up after one that did. The
   738→regrouping is worth a moment’s thought. Recovery time is a speed, so it now sits with throughput; and rework rate
   739→was added because a team could look stable while quietly spending its deployments patching production —
   740→reactive work the original four keys never surfaced.
   741→Notice the shape either way: one group measures how fast value moves, the other how safely it moves. All five are
   742→outcomes of your whole delivery system, not activities within it — which is what makes them worth watching.
   743→14.7.2 Why Paired Metrics Resist Gaming
   744→Chapter 12 introduced Goodhart’s Law (§12.1.2): when a measure becomes a target, people optimize the measure
   745→rather than the goal, and the chapter’s advice was to pair each metric with a counter-metric that degrades when
   746→someone cheats. The DORA metrics are that advice, institutionalized. Try to game throughput — deploy half-
   747→baked changes constantly — and change fail rate rises to expose you. Try to game stability — deploy once a
   748→quarter after months of manual checking — and deployment frequency and lead time collapse. Each group is the
   749→other’s counter-metric. And the fifth metric closed a cheat the original four left open: recover fast by shipping
   750→quick, unplanned patches, and recovery time looks elite while deployment rework rate exposes how much of your
   751→delivery is cleanup. A team can only improve all five together by actually getting better at delivery: smaller
   752→changes, stronger pipelines, faster recovery. There is no cheap move that improves the whole dashboard, which is
   753→the property §12.1.2 said to look for.
   754→14.7.3 What the Research Found
   755→Two findings from the DORA research deserve to reshape your intuitions. First, the spread between the best and
   756→the rest is not incremental — it is multiplicative. In the 2019 survey, elite performers deployed on demand (many
   757→times per day) where low performers deployed between once a month and once every six months; elite lead times
   758→were under a day against months; elite recovery times under an hour against a week or more — differences of
   759→orders of magnitude on the throughput measures, with change fail rates lower as well.42 The bands are re-
   760→measured each survey year and move — read them as a dated yardstick from a specific report, not a permanent
   761→threshold.
   762→Second — and this is the finding that overturned decades of folklore — speed and stability correlate
   763→positively.42 The traditional assumption was a trade-off: move fast or be careful. The data say the teams that
   764→deploy most often are also the teams that break production least and recover fastest. The mechanism should be
   765→familiar by now: high frequency forces small changes; small changes are easier to review (Chapter 9), test
   766→
   767→===== صفحة الكتاب 400 =====
   768→(Chapter 10), and attribute; attribution makes recovery fast; and fast, safe recovery removes the fear that drives
   769→batching. Slow, careful, big-batch releases are the risky choice wearing caution’s clothes.
   770→14.7.4 Measuring Your Own Five
   771→A student team can measure all five metrics with data it already has, and the exercise is worth doing because the
   772→numbers will be humbler than the elite benchmarks. Define “production” honestly — your deployed demo
   773→environment, or your instructor-facing release — then: deployment frequency is a count of deploy events per
   774→week, from your pipeline’s history. Lead time is deploy timestamp minus commit timestamp, taken as a median
   775→over recent changes (git log and your CI dashboard give you both ends). Change fail rate requires a log
   776→discipline: record each deploy and whether it needed a revert or hotfix; failures divided by deploys. Recovery
   777→time is the gap from noticing a bad deploy to restored service, from the same log. Rework rate comes from the
   778→same log too: mark each deploy planned or unplanned (a revert or hotfix shipped because production broke is
   779→unplanned); unplanned divided by total. Review the five numbers at your retrospective, and resist the urge to set
   780→targets — use them, in GQM fashion (Chapter 12), to ask why lead time is three days and which stage of your
   781→pipeline the time hides in.
   782→14.8 Conclusion
   783→Delivery is the connective tissue of everything this book has taught. The CI pipeline of §14.2 is Chapters 9 and 10
   784→made mandatory: reviews, static analysis, and tests by level, converted from practices a diligent team performs
   785→into gates no change can bypass. The DORA delivery metrics of §14.7 are Chapter 12 made honest: outcome
   786→metrics, paired against their own counter-metrics, measuring the whole system rather than rewarding activity.
   787→Continuous deployment of §14.3 is Chapter 2’s short-cycle bet made physical: the same argument that favored
   788→small iterations over big-bang phases favors small deployments over big releases, with Knight Capital and
   789→CrowdStrike as the permanent record of what happens at either failed extreme — no automation, and automation
   790→without staging. The packaging and networking of §§14.4–14.5 are that same pipeline made tangible: an image
   791→built once, a database on a durable volume, a name, a certificate, and an edge in front, so the artifact a pipeline
   792→produces actually becomes a service a user can reach. What delivery cannot do is stop the clock: the system you
   793→now ship continuously will spend years being changed, and that long tail — legacy code, refactoring, technical
   794→debt — is Chapter 15’s subject.
   795→If the chapter compresses to one sentence, it is this: make change small, make its path to users automatic and
   796→progressively exposed, and watch the outcomes.
