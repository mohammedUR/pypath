     3→     3→Chapter 12 — Quality Metrics
     4→     4→Where we are. Every earlier chapter asked you to do something well: elicit requirements, design for change,
     5→     5→review code, test thoroughly, secure it. This chapter asks a different question: how do you know whether it
     6→     6→worked? Opinions about quality are cheap and contradictory. Measurement replaces “I think the build got
     7→     7→better” with “the defect-removal efficiency rose from 84% to 91%, and here is the confidence interval.” This
     8→     8→is the most quantitative chapter in the book. We build the statistical toolkit — scales of measurement,
     9→     9→boxplots, variance, distributions, confidence intervals, and linear regression — and we ground every formula
    10→    10→in a small dataset we carry from start to finish.
    11→    11→You cannot manage what you cannot see, and in software almost nothing is directly visible. You cannot hold
    12→    12→“maintainability” in your hand or point at a cubic centimeter of “reliability.” Metrics are how we make invisible
    13→    13→attributes visible enough to reason about, compare, and improve. But metrics are also dangerous: a badly chosen
    14→    14→number will be optimized at the expense of the very thing you cared about. So this chapter is as much about
    15→    15→choosing and interpreting metrics honestly as it is about computing them. Get the statistics right, and get the
    16→    16→humility right too.

    31→    31→12.1 Meaningful Metrics
    32→    32→12.1.1 Metrics Quantify Attributes
    33→    33→An attribute is a property of some entity you care about: the size of a module, the duration of a build, the effort to
    34→    34→fix a bug, the satisfaction of a customer. A metric (more precisely, a measure) is a rule that assigns a number or
    35→    35→symbol to an attribute so that the assignment preserves something true about the world. Everything rides on that
    36→    36→last clause. If module A really is more complex than module B, then a valid complexity metric must assign A a
    37→    37→larger number than B. When the numbers stop tracking the reality, the metric has failed, no matter how carefully it
    38→    38→is computed.
    39→    39→Measurement theory names three entities you should keep distinct:
    40→    40→The entity — the thing being measured (a file, a release, a review session).
    41→    41→The attribute — the specific property of interest (its size, its defect count).
    42→    42→The metric — the mapping from attribute to value (lines of code, defects per KLOC).
    43→    43→A single entity has many attributes, and one attribute admits many metrics. A source file has size, age, complexity,
    44→    44→and churn; “size” alone can be measured as bytes, lines, statements, or tokens. Confusing these is the root of most
    45→    45→measurement nonsense. “The file is 400” is meaningless until you say 400 what, measuring which attribute.
    46→    46→Definition. A metric is valid if entities that differ in the attribute differ in the metric in the same direction (it
    47→    47→represents the attribute), and reliable if repeating the measurement gives the same value. A metric can be
    48→    48→reliable but invalid — counting lines of code is perfectly repeatable, yet it is a poor measure of “programmer
    49→    49→productivity.”
    50→    50→12.1.2 Selecting Useful Metrics
    51→    51→Since almost anything can be measured, teams must choose measures that support useful decisions. The hard part
    52→    52→is deciding what is worth measuring. A useful metric passes several tests at once:
    53→    53→1. Relevant. It moves when the attribute you care about moves. Counting comments does not track code
    54→    54→quality; if anything, heavy commenting can signal code so unclear it needs prose to explain it.
    55→    55→2. Actionable. When the number is bad, you know what to do. A metric you cannot act on is a spectator sport,
    56→    56→not an engineering tool.
    57→    57→3. Cheap enough. The cost of collecting it is small relative to the value of the decision it informs. A metric
    58→    58→that needs a week of manual effort per release will not survive contact with a deadline.
    59→    59→4. Robust against gaming. The moment a metric becomes a target for people’s incentives, people begin
    60→    60→optimizing the measured value, sometimes at the expense of the underlying goal. This is Goodhart’s Law
    61→    61→— the single most common way metrics programs go wrong.1
    62→    62→
    63→    63→===== صفحة الكتاب 316 =====
    64→    64→Pitfall. Reward developers for “lines of code written” and you will get more, longer, more duplicated code.
    65→    65→Reward testers for “number of bugs found” and you will get a flood of trivial, low-severity reports. Reward
    66→    66→“closing tickets fast” and hard tickets get closed unfixed. Every metric attached to an incentive is quietly
    67→    67→redefined by the people it measures. Prefer metrics that are hard to game and pair each metric with a
    68→    68→counter-metric that would degrade if someone cheated (pair “tickets closed” with “tickets reopened”).
    69→    69→12.1.3 Goal-Directed Measurement: GQM
    70→    70→The antidote to measuring the merely convenient is to start from a goal, not from a tool. The Goal-Question-
    71→    71→Metric (GQM) method, introduced by Victor Basili and colleagues, gives measurement a top-down discipline:2
    72→    72→Goal — a business or engineering objective, stated with an object, a purpose, a quality focus, a viewpoint,
    73→    73→and a context. “Improve (purpose) the reliability (focus) of the checkout service (object) from the
    74→    74→customer’s viewpoint (viewpoint) in production (context).”
    75→    75→Question — a concrete question whose answer would tell you whether you are meeting the goal. “Are
    76→    76→customers hitting fewer failures than last quarter?”
    77→    77→Metric — the data that answers the question. “Customer-found defects per 1,000 active installs, tracked
    78→    78→monthly.”
    79→    79→The direction matters. If you start from the metric (“we have a dashboard of 40 numbers — what do they mean?”),
    80→    80→you drown in data and act on none of it. If you start from the goal, every number on the dashboard earns its place
    81→    81→by answering a question you actually asked. When a metric answers no live question, delete it: an unused metric
    82→    82→still costs collection effort and still tempts someone to game it.

    85→    85→12.2 Software Quality
    86→    86→12.2.1 The Many Forms of Software Quality
    87→    87→“Quality” is not one thing, and arguments about software quality usually turn out to be people talking past each
    88→    88→other because they mean different forms of it. It helps to name them explicitly:
    89→    89→Functional quality — does the software do what it is supposed to do, correctly? This is conformance to
    90→    90→specification: the features work, the results are right, the edge cases are handled.
    91→    91→Process quality — is the way the software is built repeatable, visible, and improving? A team that ships
    92→    92→correct software by heroics has high functional quality and low process quality; next quarter’s release is a
    93→    93→coin flip.
    94→    94→Product quality — the internal, structural health of the artifact: readability, modularity, low coupling, test
    95→    95→coverage, absence of latent defects. Product quality is invisible to users but governs the cost of change
    96→    96→(Chapter 6).
    97→    97→Operational quality — how the system behaves in production: availability, latency, resource use,
    98→    98→recoverability. A feature-complete service that is down two hours a week has poor operational quality.
    99→    99→Aesthetic quality — the harder-to-quantify sense of a clean interface, a coherent API, a design that feels
   100→   100→right. Real, but resistant to a single number.
   101→   101→Customer-satisfaction quality — whether the people who use and pay for the software are actually happy
   102→   102→with it. This is the form that ultimately pays the bills, and it is only loosely correlated with the others: users
   103→   103→can love buggy software that solves their problem, and hate flawless software that does not.
   104→   104→The international standard ISO/IEC 25010 organizes product quality into nine characteristics — functional
   105→   105→suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability,
   106→   106→flexibility, and safety — each subdivided further. (The widely cited 2011 edition had eight, with usability and
   107→   107→portability where the current edition has interaction capability and flexibility; taxonomies get revised, which is
   108→   108→itself a lesson about measurement.)3 Memorizing the taxonomy matters less than the habit it encodes: before you
   109→   109→measure quality, say which quality.
   110→   110→Principle. There is no single “quality score.” Different stakeholders optimize different forms of quality, and
   111→   111→improving one can degrade another (heavy security hardening can hurt usability). State which form you
   112→   112→mean, whose viewpoint it takes, and why it matters — the discipline GQM enforces.
   113→   113→12.2.2 Measuring Customer Support
   114→   114→Customer support is where several forms of quality become visible at once, which makes it a rich source of
   115→   115→metrics — and a treacherous one. Common support metrics include:
   116→   116→Ticket volume — how many support requests arrive per unit time (or per install).
   117→   117→

   119→   119→Time to first response and time to resolution — how quickly the team reacts and fixes.
   120→   120→Backlog — how many open tickets, and how old the oldest are.
   121→   121→Reopen rate — the fraction of “resolved” tickets the customer reopens (a counter-metric to resolution
   122→   122→speed).
   123→   123→Customer satisfaction (CSAT) and Net Promoter Score (NPS) — survey-based measures of how
   124→   124→customers feel.4
   125→   125→Notice how each metric, alone, misleads. Falling ticket volume might mean the software improved — or that users
   126→   126→gave up and left. Fast resolution might mean skillful support — or tickets closed without really fixing anything
   127→   127→(which the reopen rate would expose). This is why support metrics travel in packs: you interpret any one of them
   128→   128→only against the others, and against the underlying goal of keeping customers successful. We will return to
   129→   129→support-driven quality improvement as a full case study in §12.5.
   130→   130→12.3 Graphical Displays of Data Sets
   131→   131→12.3.1 Data Sets
   132→   132→A data set is a collection of measured values. The simplest is univariate — one attribute measured across many
   133→   133→entities, like our eleven defect counts. A bivariate data set pairs two attributes per entity (module size and defect
   134→   134→count), which is what regression needs. Before you compute a single statistic, look at the data. A number summary
   135→   135→can hide a shape; a picture reveals it. The rest of §12.3 covers displays for categorical structure, schedules, and

   167→   167→On a nominal scale, “the average defect type is 2.3” is nonsense — the categories have no order to
   168→   168→average. You may only count and find the most frequent (the mode).
   169→   169→On an ordinal scale, you may find a median (“the median severity is High”) but not a mean, because the
   170→   170→distance from Low to Medium need not equal Medium to High.
   171→   171→On an interval scale, differences are meaningful (20°C is 10 degrees warmer than 10°C) but ratios are not
   172→   172→(20°C is not “twice as hot” as 10°C, because 0°C is not true zero). Means and standard deviations become
   173→   173→legal.
   174→   174→On a ratio scale, everything is legal, including ratios (“release B has twice the defects of release A”),
   175→   175→because zero really means “none.”
   176→   176→Pitfall. Severity ratings and Likert survey scores (“rate 1–5”) are ordinal, yet teams routinely average them.
   177→   177→“Average severity 2.7” treats the gap between severities as if it were constant, which it is not — one critical
   178→   178→defect is not “worth” three trivial ones. Report the median and the distribution of ordinal data, not the mean.
   179→   179→Our running defect counts are ratio-scaled (zero defects means none, and “twice as many” is meaningful), so
   180→   180→every statistic in this chapter is fair game for them.

   184→   184→behind one release sort by severity as: Critical 5, High 18, Medium 42, Low 15. A bar chart makes the shape
   185→   185→immediate:

   194→   194→are ordered and continuous). Confusing the two is a common error: bar charts are for categories, histograms are

   201→   201→chain of tasks that determines the earliest possible finish) visible at a glance. Here is a quality-improvement

   207→   207→12.3.5 Hill Charts: Displaying Uncertainty, Not Just Quantity
   208→   208→The progress charts most teams use — a burndown of remaining tasks, or a “percent done” bar — report the
   209→   209→quantity of work remaining, but they usually assume that the team has already discovered all of the work. The
   210→   210→riskiest work is the work you haven’t figured out yet, and a task list often grows as you discover what a problem
   211→   211→really involves. A burndown that ticks down looks reassuring right up until the unknown you never listed blows up
   212→   212→the schedule.
   213→   213→The hill chart, popularized by Basecamp’s Shape Up, adds information that a burndown omits: how much
   214→   214→uncertainty remains in each piece of work.5 Represent each item as a dot moving over a hill:

   217→   217→. . . . .            <- crest: "we know what to do"
   218→   218→.              .
   219→   219→.                    .
   220→   220→.                          .
   221→   221→___.______________________________.___
   222→   222→UPHILL                    DOWNHILL
   223→   223→"figuring it out"          "just execution"
   224→   224→(unknowns remain)          (all unknowns solved)
   225→   225→Uphill means figuring it out — open questions, unsolved design, unproven technical assumptions.
   226→   226→Downhill means just execution — the unknowns are resolved and only the known, routine work remains.
   227→   227→Two properties make it a better management signal than a burndown. First, a dot that stops moving is a raised
   228→   228→hand: it flags a stuck piece before a missed deadline announces it, and without interrogating anyone. Second, its
   229→   229→language is about the work, not the person — “what would get this scope over the hill?” depersonalizes a status
   230→   230→conversation that “why isn’t this done?” would sour. Used well, teams push the novel, risky work uphill first and
   231→   231→leave routine “screw-tightening” for last, so uncertainty falls fastest early — the same risk-first instinct behind the
   232→   232→spiral model (§2.7) and Shape Up’s building phase (§2.8).
   233→   233→Principle. Use a progress metric that makes uncertainty visible alongside the amount of work remaining. A
   234→   234→number that only counts finished tasks will look healthiest right before the unknown you never measured
   235→   235→comes due.
   236→   236→12.4 Product Quality: Measuring Defects
   237→   237→Defects are the most measurable face of quality, so they anchor most quality programs. Measure the outcome you
   238→   238→care about — usually “how much pain reaches the customer” — and avoid letting the result depend mainly on
   239→   239→how aggressively the team searched for defects.
   240→   240→12.4.1 Severity of Defects
   241→   241→Not all defects matter equally, so raw counts mislead. Teams classify each defect by severity, an ordinal scale
   242→   242→describing the impact of the failure it causes:
   243→   243→Critical (S1) — data loss, security breach, or total outage; no workaround.
   244→   244→High (S2) — a major feature is broken; a painful workaround may exist.
   245→   245→Medium (S3) — a feature misbehaves but the user can proceed.
   246→   246→Low (S4) — cosmetic or trivial; annoyance only.
   247→   247→
   248→   248→===== صفحة الكتاب 322 =====
   249→   249→Severity is distinct from priority, which is the business decision of when to fix. A low-severity typo on the login
   250→   250→page can be high-priority because everyone sees it; a critical bug in a feature no one uses can be low-priority.
   251→   251→Because severity is ordinal, summarize it with counts per level and a median — not a mean (§12.3.2). A release
   252→   252→with one Critical and five Lows can be worse than one with ten Mediums, even though the second has more total
   253→   253→defects; the severity distribution captures what the count destroys.
   254→   254→12.4.2 Defect-Removal Efficiency
   255→   255→The central product-quality metric is defect-removal efficiency (DRE): of all the defects that existed, what
   256→   256→fraction did you catch before they reached the customer?
   257→   257→DRE =
   258→   258→𝐷before
   259→   259→𝐷before + 𝐷after
   260→   260→× 100
   261→   261→where 𝐷before  is the number of defects found and removed before release (by reviews, static analysis, and testing)
   262→   262→and 𝐷after  is the number the customer finds after release. The denominator is the total defects that were ever
   263→   263→present; DRE is the share your internal nets caught.
   264→   264→Worked example. During development of a release, code review, static analysis, and testing together found
   265→   265→and removed 720 defects. In the first six months of production, customers reported 80 more.
   266→   266→DRE =
   267→   267→720
   268→   268→720 + 80 × 100
   269→   269→The team caught nine of every ten defects before shipping. Some organizations use 95% DRE as an
   270→   270→aspirational target; much lower values suggest that too many defects are escaping to users.6
   271→   271→DRE can also be computed per phase to show where defects leak through. If a phase receives defects, removes
   272→   272→some, and passes the rest downstream, its phase DRE is defects removed in the phase divided by defects present
   273→   273→when the phase began. Low phase DRE for, say, code review tells you where to invest. Two cautions keep DRE
   274→   274→trustworthy. First, you only know 𝐷after  after enough time has passed — DRE for a release is provisional until its
   275→   275→defects have had time to surface (often you fix a measurement window, like six months). Second, DRE rewards
   276→   276→finding your own bugs, so it resists the gaming that raw “bugs found” invites: inflating 𝐷before  with trivial finds
   277→   277→barely moves the ratio unless real escapes drop too.
   278→   278→12.4.3 Customer-Found Defects
   279→   279→A customer-found defect (CFD) is exactly what it sounds like: a defect first reported by a customer in
   280→   280→production, i.e., the 𝐷after  term above. CFDs are the most honest defect metric in one sense — every one
   281→   281→represents real pain that reached a real user, defects that survived every net you built. That makes CFD count, and
   282→   282→especially the severity mix of CFDs, a direct measure of escaped quality. Our running dataset is a CFD series:
   283→   283→eleven releases with 2, 4, 5, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, and 23 customer-found defects respectively in their first six
   284→   284→months.

   287→   287→12.4.4 CFDs Measure Installs, Not Quality
   288→   288→A CFD requires careful interpretation, because installation counts can make a dashboard appear healthier without
   289→   289→showing whether product quality improved. Raw CFD count is confounded by usage. A release installed on ten
   290→   290→machines and a release installed on ten million machines can have identical code quality, yet the second will
   291→   291→generate vastly more customer-found defects, simply because more users exercise more paths on more data. A
   292→   292→falling CFD count might mean the code got better — or that adoption cratered. A rising CFD count might mean
   293→   293→the code got worse — or that a hit feature tripled your install base.
   294→   294→The fix is normalization: divide by exposure. Report CFDs per 1,000 active installs, or per million transactions,
   295→   295→or per user-month. Only then does the metric track quality rather than popularity. If the release with 23 raw CFDs
   296→   296→had ten times the installs of the release with 5, it is actually the cleaner release per user.
   297→   297→Principle. Any count of production events — defects, crashes, support tickets — must be normalized by
   298→   298→exposure before you compare across releases or over time. An unnormalized production count measures how
   299→   299→many people used the software at least as much as it measures how good the software is. Raw CFDs
   300→   300→measure installs; CFDs-per-install measure quality.
   301→   301→12.5 Ops Quality Improvement: A Case Study
   302→   302→12.5.1 How to Improve Software Quality
   303→   303→You improve what you measure, measure against a target, act, and re-measure — the loop the Gantt chart in
   304→   304→§12.3.4 lays out (Measure → Analyze → Improve → Verify). This is just the scientific method wearing a hard hat,
   305→   305→and it is the most reliable way to improve quality, because it forces you to state a hypothesis (“more integration
   306→   306→tests will cut escaped defects”) and then check it against data rather than declaring victory by feeling.
   307→   307→Concretely, a hosting company we will follow — call it Northwind — ships a document service and is alarmed by
   308→   308→rising support load. Leadership wants “better quality,” which means nothing until it is operationalized. The team
   309→   309→applies GQM (§12.1.3) to turn the wish into a measurable program.
   310→   310→12.5.2 The Customer Quality Metric
   311→   311→Northwind’s goal is “reduce the reliability pain customers feel in production.” The question — “how much pain
   312→   312→reaches customers, per unit of usage?” — points to a single top-line customer quality metric:
   313→   313→𝑄cust = severity-weighted CFDs in the quarter
   314→   314→active installs (thousands)
   315→   315→.
   316→   316→Weighting by severity keeps the metric from treating a cosmetic typo like an outage (Critical = 10, High = 5,
   317→   317→Medium = 2, Low = 1, say), and normalizing by installs obeys the principle of §12.4.4. Leadership tracks this
   318→   318→

   320→   320→single value because it reflects customer impact. Since it measures outcomes instead of engineering activity, a
   321→   321→team cannot improve it simply by doing more of the wrong work.
   322→   322→12.5.3 Subgoals: Product and Process Improvement
   323→   323→A top-line outcome metric tells you whether you are winning but not how to win. So Northwind decomposes the
   324→   324→goal into two subgoals, each with its own metric:
   325→   325→Product improvement — make each release intrinsically cleaner. Metric: DRE (§12.4.2). Raising DRE
   326→   326→means fewer defects escape per release regardless of usage.
   327→   327→Process improvement — make the way releases are built more effective, so that high DRE is repeatable
   328→   328→rather than lucky. Metrics: defect-injection rate (defects introduced per KLOC), review coverage (fraction
   329→   329→of changes reviewed), and escaped-defect root-cause mix (which lifecycle phase each escaped defect
   330→   330→came from).
   331→   331→The relationship is a small hierarchy: process improvements cause product improvements (better reviews inject
   332→   332→and escape fewer defects), and product improvements cause the top-line customer metric to fall.
   333→   333→12.5.4 Measuring Process Improvements

   521→   521→The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the symmetric, bell-shaped curve defined by its mean 𝜇 and standard
   522→   522→deviation 𝜎, written 𝑁(𝜇, 𝜎2 ). It matters for two reasons. First, many natural measurements are approximately
   523→   523→normal. Second, and far more important, the Central Limit Theorem says that the average of many independent
   524→   524→measurements is approximately normal regardless of the shape of the original data — which is why the normal
   525→   525→shows up whenever you reason about sample means, even for lopsided data like our right-skewed defect counts.
   526→   526→The empirical rule (68–95–99.7) captures its spread: about 68% of values lie within 1𝜎 of the mean, about 95%
   527→   527→within 2𝜎, and about 99.7% within 3𝜎. Any value is located by its z-score, the number of standard deviations
   528→   528→from the mean:

   533→   533→minutes. A review that took 69 minutes has 𝑧= (69 −45)/12 = 2.0 — two standard deviations above the
   534→   534→mean. By the empirical rule, only about 2.5% of reviews run that long or longer, so 69 minutes is genuinely

   552→   552→12.8 Confidence Intervals
   553→   553→12.8.1 Definition of Confidence Interval
   554→   554→A sample mean is a single guess; a confidence interval is a guess with error bars. It is a range, computed from
   555→   555→your sample, that is likely to contain the true population mean. A 95% confidence interval is built by a procedure
   556→   556→that, over many repeated samples, brackets the true mean 95% of the time.
   557→   557→Pitfall. A 95% confidence interval does not mean “there is a 95% probability the true mean is in this
   558→   558→particular interval.” The true mean is a fixed (unknown) number; it is either in your interval or not. The 95%
   559→   559→describes the long-run success rate of the method, not the odds for one interval. Speak of confidence in the
   560→   560→procedure, not probability of the parameter.
   561→   561→Every confidence interval for a mean has the same shape:
   562→   562→‾𝑥± (critical value) × (standard error),
   563→   563→standard error = (SD)
   564→   564→√𝑛.
   565→   565→The standard error shrinks as √𝑛 grows: to halve your error bars you need four times the data. The only question
   566→   566→is which critical value — z or t — which depends on whether you know the population standard deviation.
   567→   567→12.8.2 If the Population Standard Deviation Is Known: z-interval
   568→   568→When 𝜎 is known (for instance, from long historical records of a stable process), use the normal distribution’s
   569→   569→critical value. For 95% confidence that value is 𝑧* = 1.96 (from the empirical rule; more precisely, 95% of a
   570→   570→normal lies within 1.96 standard deviations). The interval is:
   571→   571→‾𝑥± 𝑧* ⋅𝜎
   572→   572→√𝑛.
   573→   573→Worked example. You time 𝑛= 36 code reviews and get a sample mean of ‾𝑥= 45 minutes. From years of
   574→   574→records the population standard deviation is known to be 𝜎= 12 minutes. A 95% z-interval:
   575→   575→45 ± 1.96 ⋅12
   576→   576→√36 = 45 ± 1.96 ⋅12
   577→   577→6 = 45 ± 1.96(2) = 45 ± 3.92.

   620→   620→is pseudo-precision.

   758→   758→Pitfall. Regression measures association, not causation. A high 𝑅2  between size and defects does not prove
   759→   759→that shrinking modules causes fewer defects — both might be driven by a third factor (rushed, complex
   760→   760→features are both large and buggy). And never extrapolate: our model is fit on 2–10 KLOC and says nothing
   761→   761→reliable about a 40-KLOC module or about zero. Regression describes the data you have, in the range you
   762→   762→have it.
   763→   763→12.10 Conclusion
   764→   764→Metrics are how software engineering earns the word engineering: they replace assertion with evidence. But the
   765→   765→chapter’s two halves carry one shared lesson. The measurement half (§§12.1–12.5) insists that a number is only as
   766→   766→good as the goal behind it — choose metrics from goals (GQM), state which form of quality you mean, normalize
   767→   767→production counts by exposure so you measure quality and not popularity, and expect any metric tied to an
   768→   768→incentive to be gamed. The statistical half (§§12.6–12.9) insists that a number is only as good as its uncertainty —
   769→   769→a mean without a spread misleads, a boxplot and histogram reveal shape and outliers a mean hides, and a
   770→   770→before/after comparison without a confidence interval cannot tell a real improvement from a lucky quarter.
   771→   771→We carried one dataset through the whole apparatus: eleven customer-found-defect counts with mean 9, median 8,
   772→   772→IQR 7, variance 34.2, standard deviation 5.85, one outlier at 23, and a 95% t-interval of (5.07, 12.93); and a paired
   773→   773→dataset whose least-squares fit ̂𝑦= 2 + 1.5𝑥 explains 96% of the variation in defects by size. Every one of those
   774→   774→numbers began as an invisible attribute and ended as something you can compare, defend, and act on.
   775→   775→Used well, metrics close the loop that began in Chapter 1: they tell you, with evidence rather than opinion,
   776→   776→whether your requirements, design, reviews, and tests are actually producing software that works — and whether
   777→   777→it is getting better. Used badly, they become theater: dashboards no one reads, targets everyone games, averages
   778→   778→that hide the story. The difference is discipline, not statistical sophistication: start from a goal, say what you mean
   779→   779→by quality, and never report a change without its error bars.
