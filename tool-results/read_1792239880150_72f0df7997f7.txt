    24→14.1 SaaS and the Cloud
    25→14.1.1 From Shipped Artifact to Running Service
    26→For most of software history, delivering software meant producing an artifact — a binary on tape, a shrink-
    27→wrapped CD, an installer download — that customers took away and ran on their machines. The vendor’s job
    28→ended at the factory gate. Under that model, releases are naturally rare and heavy: every artifact that leaves the
    29→building is effectively unpatchable for months, so it must be as close to perfect as testing can make it, and
    30→mistakes are answered with recalls, patch disks, and support calls.
    31→
    32→===== صفحة الكتاب 376 =====
    33→Software as a service (SaaS) inverts the arrangement: the software runs on machines the vendor controls, and
    34→users reach it over the network, usually through a browser or a thin client. In its pure form, nothing ships. That
    35→single change rearranges almost everything downstream. The provider controls what runs in production —
    36→canaries, staged rollouts, and feature flags may briefly mix versions, but there is no museum of old customer
    37→installs to support. The vendor can observe actual usage through logs, errors, and performance data, reducing its
    38→dependence on support reports. And because the vendor owns the machines, an update is an internal operation
    39→rather than a request to thousands of customers: it can happen at any time, invisibly, many times a day.
    40→Customers also benefit from the move toward SaaS: their data lives on the service, so a stolen laptop or a dead
    41→phone loses a device, not the work; several people can collaborate on the same data at once instead of emailing
    42→copies back and forth; and datasets that are large or constantly changing can live in one central, managed place
    43→rather than being squeezed onto whatever machine each user happens to own. For a whole class of applications —
    44→shared documents, shared calendars, anything with one truth that many people touch — the service model is both
    45→cheaper to operate and a better product.
    46→On the user’s side of the wire, the near-universal thin client is the browser: a program every device already has,
    47→speaking a front-end stack of HTML for the document’s structure, CSS for its presentation, and JavaScript for
    48→behavior that runs on the client — while the server side can be written in whatever language the team prefers,
    49→because the browser never sees it. Native mobile apps are the sibling front end, trading the browser’s universality
    50→for tighter platform integration; either way, the architecture is the same service behind a thin client.
    51→14.1.2 Multi-Tenancy and Horizontal Scaling
    52→Running the software yourself raises a design question: does each customer get their own copy? In a multi-tenant
    53→design, one running instance of the system serves many customers — tenants — whose data is kept logically
    54→separate inside shared infrastructure. Multi-tenancy is what makes SaaS economical: one fleet, one deployment,
    55→one upgrade for everyone, with the cost of operations amortized across all tenants. The price is that isolation
    56→becomes a software problem. A bug that leaks one tenant’s data to another, or one tenant’s heavy load that starves
    57→the rest (the “noisy neighbor”), are failure modes a shipped artifact simply could not have.
    58→Serving many tenants also means serving unpredictable load, and the cloud’s answer is horizontal scaling:
    59→instead of buying a bigger machine (vertical scaling), you run more copies of the same service behind a load
    60→balancer and add or remove copies as demand moves. Horizontal scaling only works cleanly if any copy can serve
    61→any request — which is exactly why the statelessness convention of RESTful design matters (§7.5.4). A service
    62→that keeps per-client session state on one server has silently welded each user to that server; a stateless one lets the
    63→platform treat servers as interchangeable, replaceable cattle. Architecture decisions from Chapter 7 are, it turns
    64→out, delivery decisions too.

    67→14.1.3 What the Cloud Does to the Iron Triangle
    68→Recall the iron triangle of Chapter 1 (§1.5): scope, schedule, and cost constrain each other, and the classic pressure
    69→point was the release date — a single, high-stakes deadline toward which scope was cut and quality was crunched.
    70→SaaS dissolves that pressure point. When deploying is cheap and continuous, release stops being an event and
    71→becomes a decision — a small, revocable, per-feature decision made many times a week. You no longer ask
    72→“what can we finish by the ship date?” but “is this one change ready to meet users now?” Schedule pressure does
    73→not vanish; it decomposes into many tiny pressures, each too small to justify heroics.
    74→That is the economic root of the deploy-early culture. Teams that deploy each small change as it is finished get
    75→feedback while the change is still fresh in their heads, keep the gap between “works on my machine” and “works
    76→in production” permanently small, and never build up a terrifying pile of unreleased work whose first contact with
    77→reality is a big bang — the same pathology, at the release level, that §2.4.1 diagnosed at the integration level. The
    78→practices in the remainder of this chapter reduce the risk of early deployment.
    79→14.1.4 The Cloud Landscape: Providers, Components, and Responsibility
    80→The vendor-controlled machines of §14.1.1 have to live somewhere, and today they mostly live in a public cloud:
    81→vast provider-owned datacenters whose capacity is rented out over the network, by the hour or by the request. As
    82→of 2025, the market is led by Amazon Web Services (AWS), Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure, with a long tail
    83→of smaller providers — DigitalOcean, Linode, Hetzner — that trade breadth of catalog for simplicity and price.1
    84→Hold the names loosely: provider names and product lists date quickly, but the concepts beneath them do not.
    85→Every cloud, whatever its branding, sells the same four component families. Compute is processing capacity —
    86→virtual machines, containers, or functions that run your code. Storage is durable data — disks, object stores,
    87→managed databases. Network is the plumbing — private networks, load balancers, DNS — that lets those pieces
    88→reach each other and the world. And security and identity is the control plane over the other three: who, and
    89→which programs, may do what to which resources.
    90→The cloud’s economic promise is threefold. You pay only for what you use, turning a large up-front hardware
    91→purchase into a metered utility bill. You can place your system in regions around the world, near your users,
    92→without signing a lease on another continent. And the hardware is someone else’s problem — provisioning, failed
    93→disks, power, cooling, and physical security are all handled below the waterline of the service you rent.
    94→What the provider explicitly does not handle is captured by the shared responsibility model: the provider secures
    95→the cloud — the facilities, the hardware, the hypervisor — while you secure what is in it — your operating-system
    96→configuration, your code, your data, and who can access all three.2 This is a legal line as much as a practical one,
    97→and teams forget it at their peril: a world-readable storage bucket full of customer records is your breach, however
    98→secure the datacenter around it was.
    99→None of this makes the cloud automatic. The alternatives — on-premises servers you own, or rented space and
   100→machines in a shared datacenter (co-location) — never went away, and the honest question is when each wins.
   101→
   102→===== صفحة الكتاب 378 =====
   103→Cloud makes clear sense when you do not yet know what hardware you need, when demand is spiky or
   104→unpredictable (provision for the spike and pay for it forever, or scale for the hour it lasts), when speed to a first
   105→MVP matters more than unit cost, and when a small team has no one to spare for operations.
   106→Case study. Cloud repatriation. The trade cuts the other way, too. In 2022–23, 37signals — the company
   107→behind Basecamp — publicly moved its products off the cloud and onto purchased hardware, documenting
   108→seven-figure annual savings for workloads that were steady and predictable rather than spiky.3 4 And
   109→Amazon’s own Prime Video team published an account of cutting the cost of one audio/video monitoring
   110→service by roughly 90 percent — by moving it away from a serverless, distributed-microservices design and
   111→back into a monolith-style process.5 None of this means the cloud is over. The cloud is a cost-and-flexibility
   112→trade, not an axiom. Elastic, uncertain load is where renting wins; steady, predictable load is where owning
   113→wins. Run the numbers, not the fashion.
   114→14.1.5 Containers, Clusters, and Kubernetes
   115→The unit of cloud deployment that has won in practice is the container: a package containing an application plus
   116→everything it needs to run — language runtime, libraries, configuration — isolated from other software on the
   117→same machine while sharing the host operating system’s kernel. Sharing the kernel is what distinguishes a
   118→container from a virtual machine, which carries an entire guest operating system of its own: a container starts in
   119→seconds rather than minutes, and a single host can run dozens of them.6 The container image is the immutable,
   120→versioned artifact that a CI pipeline (§14.2.2) builds exactly once and then deploys everywhere — the same byte-
   121→for-byte thing on a laptop, in the test environment, and in production, which retires “works on my machine” as a
   122→category of excuse.
   123→One host is rarely enough, so containers are run on a cluster: a set of network-connected machines managed as a
   124→single pool of compute, storage, and memory. And the de-facto standard software for managing that pool is
   125→Kubernetes (84% of organizations in CNCF’s 2023 survey were using or evaluating it), often described as the
   126→operating system for the cloud: just as an OS schedules processes onto CPU cores, Kubernetes schedules
   127→containers onto the machines of a cluster.7 Around that core job it handles ingress (routing incoming traffic to the
   128→right containers), scaling the number of running copies up and down with demand, restarting containers that crash
   129→or fail health checks, and attaching storage to containers that need it — the operational chores of §14.1.2’s
   130→horizontal scaling, automated.
   131→One caveat: Kubernetes earns its considerable complexity at fleet scale. For a small team — certainly for a class
   132→project — a single container on one host, or a platform-as-a-service that runs your container for you, delivers most
   133→of the benefit at a small fraction of the operational cost. Learn what Kubernetes is for; reach for it when you have
   134→the problem it solves.

   137→14.1.6 Distributed Trade-Offs: The CAP Theorem
   138→Horizontal scaling (§14.1.2) eventually reaches the data layer, and the moment state is replicated — living on
   139→more than one machine — a hard theoretical limit applies. Eric Brewer’s CAP theorem concerns three properties
   140→of a distributed data system: consistency (every read sees the most recent write), availability (every request
   141→receives a response), and partition tolerance (the system keeps operating when the network splits and some
   142→machines cannot reach others).8 Its real content is narrower than the popular “pick two of three”: in the presence
   143→of a partition, a replicated system cannot guarantee both strong consistency and availability, so for that data it
   144→must choose which of the two to weaken until the network heals.9
   145→Since partitions are a fact of real networks — switches fail, cables get cut, datacenters lose connectivity —
   146→partition tolerance is not really optional, and the practical content of the theorem is a forced choice during a
   147→partition: consistency or availability. A replica that cannot reach the others can either refuse to answer (staying
   148→consistent but unavailable) or answer from possibly stale data (staying available but inconsistent). Which to
   149→sacrifice is a requirements question, not a technical one. A bank chooses consistency: better to refuse service for a
   150→minute than to show two customers different balances for the same account. A social feed chooses availability: a
   151→like count that is a few seconds stale harms no one, but an unreachable feed is a product failure.
   152→System

   161→The theorem is why architecture and delivery keep meeting in this chapter: the shared-data pattern (§7.2.1) put

   163→which point CAP stops being theory and becomes a decision your team must make on purpose, per feature, with
   164→the requirements in hand.
   165→14.2 Continuous Integration Pipelines
   166→Chapter 2 introduced continuous integration (CI) as an XP engineering practice: everyone merges into a shared
   167→mainline many times a day, and an automated build-and-test run verifies each merge (§2.3.4). That definition told
   168→you what CI is and why it exists — to surface integration mismatches one small change at a time instead of all at
   169→once. This section goes deeper: what the automation actually consists of, what branching discipline it demands,
   170→and what social contract makes it work.
   171→14.2.1 Trunk-Based Development
   172→CI is first a branching policy, and only second a server. In trunk-based development, the whole team commits to
   173→a single shared mainline (the trunk), directly or through short-lived branches that live hours to a couple of days
   174→

   176→before merging. The alternative — long-lived feature branches, where each developer or feature camps on its
   177→own branch for weeks — feels safer because nobody’s half-done work disturbs anyone else. But the safety is an
   178→illusion with interest due at merge time: the longer two branches evolve apart, the more their eventual merge
   179→resembles the big-bang integration of §2.4.1, with conflicting assumptions surfacing all at once, weeks after they
   180→were made.
   181→Integration becomes more difficult as branches diverge, and divergence increases with time. Trunk-based
   182→development keeps divergence permanently small by construction. Merging several times a day means each merge
   183→carries at most a few hours of divergence — small enough that conflicts are rare, and trivial when they occur. The
   184→obvious objection — “how do I commit work that isn’t finished?” — has a standard answer you will meet in
   185→§14.3.3: hide unfinished work behind a feature flag so it can be integrated without being released. Integration
   186→and release become independent decisions, which is one of the most useful separations in this whole chapter.
   187→14.2.2 The Stages of a Pipeline
   188→A CI pipeline is the automated gauntlet every commit runs before it is declared good. The pipeline is where
   189→earlier chapters’ verification techniques stop being activities a diligent person might perform and become gates no
   190→change can skip. A typical pipeline runs stages in increasing order of cost, failing fast on the cheap ones:

   199→matters more than it looks: the artifact you tested is byte-for-byte the artifact you will deploy. Rebuilding “the

   207→14.2.3 Broken-Build Discipline
   208→A pipeline is only as good as the team’s response when it turns red. The working culture of CI rests on a small
   209→social contract. First, a red mainline is everyone’s emergency: when the trunk build breaks, fixing it (or
   210→reverting the breaking commit) takes priority over new work, because every hour the build stays red, every other
   211→developer is either blocked or building on sand. Second, never commit onto a broken build — you would be
   212→stacking unverified change on unverified change, exactly the divergence CI exists to prevent. Third, do not leave
   213→a broken build overnight; revert if you cannot fix quickly. Reverting is not an insult. It is the cheap, always-
   214→available path back to a known-good state, and teams that treat it as routine stay green far more than teams that
   215→treat it as defeat.
   216→A broken build interrupts the entire team’s work and should be treated as an urgent operational problem. While it
   217→is green, every developer has a continuous, machine-checked guarantee that the shared codebase works and can
   218→build on top of it; while it is red, nobody knows whether the system works, and restoring it comes before other
   219→work.
   220→Pitfall. The flaky test. A test that fails intermittently without a code change is more corrosive than a test that
   221→always fails, because it teaches the team to ignore red. Once “just re-run it, that one’s flaky” enters the
   222→vocabulary, the build has become a slot machine, and real failures start slipping through on the same shrug.
   223→Quarantine flaky tests immediately, fix or delete them promptly, and treat their existence as a defect in the
   224→suite. §10.2.4 catalogs the usual causes — races, shared state, order dependence, unstable externals — and
   225→their fixes.
   226→14.2.4 Keeping Pipelines Fast
   227→Pipeline speed is a hard constraint, not a convenience. Developers are supposed to merge several times a day and
   228→to wait for green before moving on. If the pipeline takes an hour, they will not wait — they will batch up bigger
   229→changes to amortize the wait, which re-creates the large, risky merges CI exists to eliminate. A useful target is
   230→roughly ten minutes from push to verdict for the merge-blocking stages.10
   231→Achieving that is the testing pyramid of §10.2.4 applied as an engineering budget: push checks down the pyramid,
   232→where they are fast, and keep the slow end-to-end layer thin. Beyond that, run independent stages in parallel,
   233→cache dependencies and build outputs so unchanged parts are not rebuilt, and split the pipeline into a fast merge-
   234→blocking core plus deeper suites (full end-to-end runs, performance tests, long fuzzing) that run continuously
   235→against the trunk without holding up merges.
   236→

   238→Order matters inside the merge-blocking core, too. The pipeline’s verdict arrives when the first test fails, not when
   239→the last one passes — so run the tests most likely to fail first: the ones nearest the changed code, and the ones that
   240→have failed most recently. At the largest scale, teams select per change the subset of tests worth running at all — a
   241→budgeted-selection problem (the knapsack problem in disguise) that §10.1.6 examines, and that industrial CI
   242→systems implement as predictive test selection. Treat a slow pipeline as a process defect that changes how your
   243→team behaves, not as a tooling annoyance to live with.
   244→14.3 Continuous Deployment
   245→14.3.1 Delivery versus Deployment
   246→Two similar terms name genuinely different commitments. Continuous delivery means every change that passes
   247→the pipeline yields an artifact that is proven deployable — the release decision is a business choice, but it is always
   248→available, at the push of a button, with no additional engineering work. Continuous deployment goes one step
   249→further: every change that passes the pipeline is deployed to production automatically, with no human in the loop.
   250→Delivery makes release possible at any moment; deployment makes it actual at every moment.
   251→Continuous deployment sounds reckless until you notice what it forbids. If every green commit goes to
   252→production, then there is no such thing as a “safe to merge but not ready to ship” change without a flag, no manual
   253→pre-release checklist to lean on, and no batching of changes into a big release whose failures cannot be attributed.
   254→Every safety property must be automated, because automation is all there is. Teams that adopt it report a
   255→paradoxical result that §14.7 will make precise: deploying more often makes each deployment less risky, because
   256→each one is smaller, better attributed, and easier to undo.
   257→14.3.2 Deployment Strategies
   258→However often you deploy, how you swap new code into a live system determines the blast radius when
   259→something is wrong. Two infrastructure-level strategies dominate practice; a third approach moves the switch into
   260→code, and it gets its own section (§14.3.3).
   261→Blue-green deployment runs two identical production environments. At any moment one (say, blue) serves all
   262→traffic while the other (green) sits idle. To release, you deploy the new version to the idle environment, verify it
   263→there against production conditions, then switch the router so all traffic flows to it. The old environment stays
   264→warm, so if the new version misbehaves, recovery is one router change back. You pay for this in doubled
   265→infrastructure and in the care demanded by anything stateful: a database schema shared by both environments
   266→must remain compatible with both versions during the switch.
   267→Canary deployment releases the new version to a small slice of traffic first — one server, one percent of users,
   268→one region — and watches error rates, latency, and business metrics before widening. The name comes from the
   269→caged canary that warned miners of gas: the small exposed population absorbs the harm and sounds the alarm
   270→
   271→===== صفحة الكتاب 384 =====
   272→while the damage is still bounded. Large operators generalize canaries into staged rings: the change rolls to ring
   273→after ring — internal users, then a small public slice, then broader populations — with automated health checks
   274→gating each promotion and halting the rollout on regression. The essential idea is progressive exposure: no change
   275→reaches everyone until it has demonstrably survived contact with someone.
   276→14.3.3 Feature Flags: Decoupling Deploy from Release
   277→Blue-green and canary deployments control exposure with infrastructure — routers, server pools, traffic slices. A
   278→feature flag (or feature toggle) moves the same control into code: a runtime conditional that turns a code path on
   279→or off without redeploying.11 Deploying and releasing become fully independent acts. A feature’s code can sit in
   280→production for weeks, dark and disabled, while the team keeps merging; when the moment comes, “releasing” it is
   281→a configuration change that takes effect in seconds, and un-releasing it is the same change in reverse. This is the
   282→endpoint of §14.1.3’s argument: release is no longer even a deployment decision — it is a bit you flip.
   283→Flags are also the standard answer to §14.2.1’s objection (“how do I commit work that isn’t finished?”):
   284→unfinished work merges to trunk dark and disabled, so integration stays continuous while release waits. In practice
   285→flags come in a few kinds with very different lifespans, and confusing them is where trouble starts:
   286→Release flags hide work in progress until it is ready. They should live days to weeks — a release flag whose
   287→feature has fully rolled out is a deletion you owe.
   288→Operational flags — including the kill switch — guard risky paths so an operator can disable a
   289→misbehaving feature instantly, without a deploy. These are deliberately long-lived, few in number, and
   290→tested like the safety equipment they are.
   291→Experiment flags split traffic between variants so you can measure a change (the A/B tests your metrics
   292→chapter made honest — §12.5). They live exactly as long as the experiment.
   293→Targeting is what makes flags more than an on/off switch: a flag can be on for one user, one tenant, or three
   294→percent of traffic — a percentage rollout, which is canary deployment’s progressive-exposure idea (§14.3.2)
   295→implemented in code, with no second environment to pay for.

   335→14.3.4 Rollback versus Roll-Forward
   336→When a deployment goes wrong, you have two exits. Rollback returns production to the previous version; roll-
   337→forward ships a new fix on top of the broken state. Rollback is usually faster and requires no new (unverified)
   338→code, so mature teams treat it as the default reflex. But a rollback path is a mechanism, and Chapter 10’s lesson
   339→applies to mechanisms too: an untested rollback is a rumor, not a capability. Version skew can make the old code
   340→unable to read data the new code wrote; a config change may have accompanied the code; the “previous artifact”
   341→may no longer exist. Teams that take this seriously rehearse rollback routinely — some by making it the normal
   342→end of every canary that fails a health check, so the path is exercised weekly rather than discovered during a crisis.
   343→And some changes cannot be rolled back at all (an irreversible data migration, a security fix you must not un-
   344→ship), which is why roll-forward speed — how fast your pipeline can carry a one-line fix to production — is itself
   345→a safety property.
