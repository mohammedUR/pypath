     3→Chapter 3 — User Requirements
     4→Where we are. Chapter 1 argued that the hardest part of many projects is deciding which system to build,
     5→and Chapter 2 gave you a process to build it in short, adaptive iterations. This chapter zooms in on the front
     6→of that process: how you discover what real people need, how you write it down so a team can act on it, and
     7→how you keep those written needs honest as understanding shifts. Everything downstream — design, tests,
     8→metrics — inherits the quality of the requirements you produce here. Get this wrong and you will build a
     9→beautiful, well-tested answer to the wrong question.
    10→A requirement is the bridge between a person with a problem and a team with the skill to solve it. That bridge is
    11→where most projects quietly succeed or fail. You can recover from a bad database choice; you refactor it. You
    12→rarely recover cheaply from having built the wrong feature for six months, because the cost is not just the wasted
    13→code — it is the wasted time, the eroded trust of the people who told you what they wanted and watched you miss
    14→it. This chapter is about narrowing that gap between what people need and what you believe they need, and doing
    15→so continuously, because the gap never fully closes.
    16→3.1 What Is a Requirement?
    17→A requirement is a statement of something the software must do for its users, or a quality it must have while
    18→doing it. That sounds simple, and the trouble hides in the simplicity. Requirements are written in natural language,
    19→by and for humans, about needs that the humans themselves often cannot articulate. They are simultaneously the
    20→most important artifact in a project and the least precise. Learning to work skillfully in that tension — to extract
    21→precision from ambiguity without pretending the ambiguity is gone — is the core skill of this chapter.
    22→Definition. A requirement is an agreed statement of a need that a stakeholder has of the system: either a
    23→behavior the system must exhibit or a property it must possess. A requirement is not a design; it says what
    24→and why, not how.
    25→That last sentence matters more than it looks. When a user says “add a dropdown,” they have handed you a design
    26→dressed up as a need. Your job is to reach past it to the need underneath — “I need to pick one clinic from a short
    27→list without typos” — because the need admits better designs than the one the user happened to imagine.
    28→Requirements work is largely the discipline of not accepting the first solution offered as if it were the problem.
    29→3.1.1 The Basic Requirements Cycle
    30→Requirements are not gathered once and filed away. They move through a repeating cycle, and the health of a
    31→project is largely the health of this loop. You elicit needs from stakeholders, analyze and organize them into
    32→

    34→something coherent, specify them in a form the team can act on, and validate that what you specified is actually
    35→what people need — which surfaces new needs and misunderstandings, sending you around again.
    36→Notice two things about this diagram. First, the loop never terminates during an active project; it merely slows as
    37→understanding stabilizes. Second, Build feeds back into Elicit. Users learn what they want by using something, so
    38→the most powerful requirements-elicitation tool you own is often a working (or even fake) version of the product.
    39→This is why Chapter 2’s iterative process and this chapter’s requirements work are the same activity viewed at two
    40→zoom levels: each iteration is one trip around this cycle.

    43→Principle. Requirements are discovered, not collected. You do not walk into a room and receive a complete
    44→list; you co-create understanding with stakeholders over time, and the earliest versions are always partly
    45→wrong. Plan for that, don’t resent it.
    46→3.1.2 Requirements Challenges
    47→Why is this hard? Because every step of the cycle fights human nature and the limits of language. A handful of
    48→recurring challenges account for most requirements failures:
    49→Tacit knowledge. Experts cannot fully articulate what they know. A nurse triaging patients follows rules
    50→she has never written down and would struggle to state. Ask “how do you decide?” and you get a
    51→simplified, partly fictional answer. The real rules live in practice, not in interviews.
    52→The unstated obvious. Stakeholders omit what is obvious to them. “Of course the system must handle two
    53→clinicians sharing a patient” goes unsaid — until it breaks.
    54→Solutions masquerading as needs. As above, users hand you designs. Accept them uncritically and you
    55→inherit everything the user overlooked.
    56→Conflicting stakeholders. The front desk wants speed; compliance wants audit trails; the sponsor wants
    57→low cost. These pull in different directions, and no phrasing makes the conflict disappear. Someone must
    58→decide, and requirements work makes the conflict visible enough to decide well.
    59→Ambiguous language. “The report should load quickly.” How quickly? For whom? Under what load?
    60→“Quickly” is a placeholder for an argument no one has had yet.
    61→Change. Even a perfectly captured requirement expires. Regulations update, markets move, users mature.
    62→A frozen specification is a specification that is slowly going stale.
    63→None of these are solved by working harder at the same technique. They are managed by combining techniques —
    64→interviews plus observation plus prototypes plus review — so that what one misses another catches.
    65→3.1.3 Kinds of Requirements
    66→It helps enormously to sort requirements into two families, because they are elicited, written, and tested in
    67→different ways.
    68→A functional requirement describes a behavior: something the system does in response to input. “When a patient
    69→checks in, the system marks their appointment as arrived and notifies the assigned clinician.” You can usually test
    70→a functional requirement with a concrete example: given this input, expect this output. Most user stories (§3.4)
    71→capture functional requirements.
    72→A non-functional requirement — better called a quality requirement — describes a property the system must
    73→have while it behaves: how fast, how secure, how available, how usable, how maintainable. “Check-in must
    74→complete in under one second for 95% of requests when 200 clinics are active.” Quality requirements are the ones
    75→
    76→===== صفحة الكتاب 67 =====
    77→teams most often botch, because they are easy to state vaguely (“it should be fast”) and hard to state testably (“p95
    78→latency under 1s at 200 concurrent clinics”). The discipline is to attach a measurable fit criterion to every quality
    79→requirement, so that “done” is a fact and not an argument.1
    80→Pitfall. Quality requirements are frequently invisible until violated. No one writes a story titled “the app
    81→should not leak patient data,” so no one budgets for it, and it surfaces only as an incident. Make quality
    82→requirements explicit and measurable, or they will be discovered by your users on your worst day.

    85→3.2 Developing Requirements and Software
    86→How you handle requirements depends on how you build software, and the two dominant philosophies — agile
    87→and plan-driven — take genuinely different stances. Neither is universally right. Understanding why each makes
    88→sense in its context lets you choose deliberately rather than by fashion.
    89→3.2.1 Agile Methods Validate Working Software
    90→Agile methods treat requirements as a hypothesis to be tested by shipping. Rather than try to specify everything up
    91→front — which the challenges in §3.1.2 say you cannot do reliably — agile teams specify a little, build it, put it in
    92→front of users, and let their reaction correct the next round. The unit of truth is working software, not a document.
    93→If the demo delights the clinic staff, the requirement was right; if they frown, you learn cheaply and adjust before
    94→you have built a mountain on a bad foundation.
    95→This is a bet, and the bet is that feedback is more reliable than foresight. In domains where users cannot tell you
    96→what they want until they see it — which is most consumer and business software — the bet usually pays off. The
    97→cost is that you must be genuinely willing to change: an agile team that treats its first backlog as fixed has adopted
    98→the ceremony without the substance.
    99→3.2.2 An Agile Emphasis on Requirements
   100→A common misreading is that agile means “no requirements.” The opposite is true. Agile teams often do more
   101→requirements work than plan-driven teams — they just do it in small, continuous doses rather than one large batch.
   102→Every iteration begins with refining and selecting requirements (the backlog), and ends with validating them
   103→against a running system. Requirements conversations happen constantly, at the moment they are needed, with the
   104→person who has the answer in the room.
   105→The agile move is to shift requirements from a document to a conversation plus a thin written reminder. A user
   106→story (§3.4) is deliberately too short to be a full specification; it is a promise to have a conversation later, when
   107→the team is about to build it and knows the most it will ever know.2 This defers the detailed decisions to the last
   108→responsible moment, when they are cheapest and best-informed.
   109→Principle. In agile work, the written requirement is a placeholder for a conversation, not a substitute for it.
   110→The story reminds you what to talk about; the value is created when the developer, the customer, and the
   111→tester talk it through together — the “three amigos.”3
   112→3.2.3 Plan-Driven Methods Validate a Specification
   113→Plan-driven (sometimes called “specification-first” or, historically, “waterfall”) methods invest heavily in writing
   114→a complete, precise, agreed specification before significant building begins. Validation happens against the
   115→
   116→===== صفحة الكتاب 69 =====
   117→document: reviews, sign-offs, and traceability checks confirm that the specification is complete and consistent,
   118→and later that the built system matches it.
   119→This looks old-fashioned next to agile, and for a scheduling app it usually is. But there are domains where it is
   120→exactly right. When the cost of a late change is enormous or the system is safety-critical — avionics, medical
   121→devices, spacecraft, systems bound by a regulatory certification — you cannot “ship and see.” You must reason
   122→carefully about the requirements up front, because a failure in the field is catastrophic and a change after
   123→certification is ruinously expensive. In those worlds, a rigorous specification is the cheapest place to catch a life-
   124→threatening mistake.
   125→Most real projects blend the two: a stable, carefully specified core (the things that must not be wrong) surrounded
   126→by an agile, iterative shell (the things you will learn by trying). Choosing where to draw that line is an engineering
   127→judgment, and the right cut depends on the cost of being wrong.
   128→3.3 Eliciting User Needs
   129→Elicitation is the active work of drawing out needs that stakeholders cannot simply hand you. The word is chosen
   130→carefully: you elicit needs the way a good teacher elicits an answer — with questions, prompts, and situations that
   131→help people discover what they know but had not stated.
   132→3.3.1 A Classification of Needs
   133→Not all needs are equal, and it helps to classify them by how the user relates to them. A useful three-way split:
   134→Stated needs — what the user explicitly asks for. “I want to search appointments by patient name.” These
   135→are the easiest to collect and the most likely to be half-solutions.
   136→Implied needs — what the user assumes without saying, because it is obvious to them. “Of course the
   137→search shouldn’t show patients from other clinics.” Miss these and users feel the system is broken even
   138→though it does everything they said.
   139→Latent needs — what the user does not know they want until they see it, but which delights them when
   140→they do. “Oh — it can flag patients who haven’t confirmed? I didn’t know I needed that, but now I can’t
   141→live without it.”
   142→The gap between stated and implied needs is where most “the software technically works but everyone hates it”
   143→failures live. The latent needs are where competitive advantage lives. A team that only satisfies stated needs builds
   144→something adequate; a team that uncovers implied and latent needs builds something people love.
   145→3.3.2 Accessing User Needs
   146→There is no single technique that reaches all three kinds of needs, so skilled teams triangulate. Each of the main
   147→methods has a characteristic strength and a matching blind spot:
   148→
   149→===== صفحة الكتاب 70 =====
   150→Interviews. Direct and flexible; great for stated needs and stakeholder goals. Weakness: people rationalize
   151→and misremember what they actually do. What people say they do and what they do often differ.
   152→Observation (contextual inquiry).4 Watch users do the real task in their real environment. Superb for tacit
   153→and implied needs — you see the sticky note on the monitor, the workaround, the double-entry no one
   154→mentioned. Weakness: expensive, and observation can change behavior.
   155→Surveys. Cheap at scale; good for prioritizing among known options and quantifying how common a need
   156→is. Weakness: useless for discovering needs you did not already think to ask about.
   157→Prototypes and demos. Show something concrete and watch the reaction. The single best tool for latent
   158→needs, because a person who cannot describe what they want can almost always react to what is in front of
   159→them. Weakness: users may fixate on the prototype’s incidental choices.
   160→Brainstorming (blue-sky sessions). Gather many stakeholders and think big: during the session, no idea is
   161→ruled out or criticized, and everything is captured — the goal is quantity first, judgment second, because
   162→premature filtering kills the unusual idea that turns out to matter. Keep the focus on the core of what the
   163→software is supposed to do, then reconvene later, with cooler heads, to filter and prioritize what the session
   164→produced. Weakness: without that second, critical pass, a brainstorm yields a wish list, not requirements.
   165→Role play. A developer pretends to be the software while the customer walks through using it: “I’d click
   166→here… no, it should already show today’s schedule.” The customer’s instructions — and especially their
   167→corrections — are requirements spoken aloud. Write everything down during the session, then split the
   168→notes afterward into requirements and open questions. Weakness: you only exercise the workflows the
   169→customer thinks to perform.
   170→Studying existing systems and data. Support tickets, competitor products, and usage logs are elicitation
   171→gold — they are records of real needs and real friction, unfiltered by anyone’s memory.
   172→Use several elicitation methods, because each corrects a different weakness: interviews form hypotheses,
   173→observation tests them, prototypes reveal omissions, and surveys help prioritize the findings.
   174→Case study. A team building the clinic scheduler interviewed front-desk staff, who asked for faster search.
   175→Only when the team watched a morning rush did they see the real problem: staff kept a paper list of
   176→“difficult” patients beside the keyboard because the system had no way to flag them. No interview surfaced
   177→this — it was too normal to mention. Observation found in one hour what interviews had missed for weeks.
   178→Frame the problem before the solution. Users — and stakeholders — hand you solutions (“add a dropdown,”
   179→“redesign the reports page”). A useful habit from Basecamp’s Shape Up is to flip every such request from “what
   180→should we build?” back to “what’s really going wrong?” — the specific moment in the workflow where today’s
   181→experience fails.5 Two ideas sharpen that:
   182→The baseline is what users do today without the feature. Stated plainly (“right now they keep a paper list
   183→beside the keyboard”), it both proves the problem is real and becomes the reference for judging any
   184→solution: a fix only has to beat the baseline, not reach perfection.

   187→A grab-bag is the anti-pattern — a vague mandate (“do something about scheduling”) with no driving
   188→problem, so no one can tell where it starts or ends. Grab-bags are how scope explodes; the cure is to narrow
   189→to one concrete pain point before designing.
   190→Pitfall. A requirement phrased as a solution smuggles in an unexamined assumption about how. Trace it
   191→back to the problem and you often find a smaller, cheaper, better answer than the one you were asked for.
   192→Whatever mix of techniques you use, there will always be a gap between what the customer said and what you
   193→understood — and that gap fills itself with assumptions. Every unstated assumption buried in a story is untracked
   194→risk: you will build on it, and if it is wrong, everything on top of it is suspect.
   195→Technique — track your assumptions. Write assumptions down explicitly, the moment you notice yourself
   196→making one (“we assume clinicians never edit each other’s appointments”). Then convert each into a
   197→question for the customer at the next meeting — an assumption is just a question you have not asked yet. If
   198→an assumption cannot be clarified yet, treat that as a prioritization signal: deprioritize the work that depends
   199→on it until it can be, and spend the iteration on stories that stand on firmer ground (see the prioritization
   200→techniques in Chapter 4, §4.4).
   201→3.3.3 Design for Delight
   202→Satisfying stated needs makes a product acceptable. Delighting users — meeting latent needs they did not know to
   203→ask for — makes a product loved, and loved products tend to win. A useful mental model here — the Kano
   204→model,6 developed fully in §4.5 — distinguishes categories of features by how user satisfaction responds to them:
   205→Must-be features (basic expectations). Their presence is unnoticed; their absence is a dealbreaker. A
   206→scheduler that loses appointments has failed no matter how pretty it is. These map to implied needs. You get
   207→no credit for them, but you cannot skip them.
   208→Performance features (the more, the better). Satisfaction scales with how well you do them: faster search,
   209→more report options. These map to stated needs. This is where users compare products, so doing them well
   210→matters.
   211→Delighters (unexpected value). Their absence is not missed, because no one expected them — but their
   212→presence produces disproportionate delight. These map to latent needs. The patient-confirmation flag above
   213→is a delighter.
   214→

   216→Pitfall. Delighters decay into expectations. The first scheduler with automatic reminders delighted users;
   217→today a scheduler without reminders feels broken. What was a delighter becomes a must-be. Elicitation
   218→continues throughout development because user expectations rise as products and competitors improve.
   219→Two practical consequences. First, spend your must-be budget ruthlessly but seek no glory there — just don’t fail.
   220→Second, invest deliberately in one or two delighters per release, because they are where love and loyalty are
   221→earned, and they are precisely the needs your competitors also failed to ask about.
   222→3.4 Writing Requirements: Stories and Features
   223→Having elicited needs, you must write them down in a form the whole team can act on. Two complementary
   224→formats dominate modern practice: user stories (small, user-centered, conversational) and system features (larger
   225→capabilities that group related stories).
   226→3.4.1 Guidelines for Effective User Stories
   227→A user story is a short statement of a need from a user’s point of view, written to provoke a conversation rather
   228→than to settle every detail. The most widely used template, often called the Connextra format after the company
   229→where it originated,7 is:
   230→
   231→===== صفحة الكتاب 73 =====
   232→As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>.
   233→For example:
   234→As a front-desk clerk, I want to flag a patient as “needs interpreter,” so that the clinician is prepared before
   235→the visit begins.
   236→Each clause has a job. The role keeps you honest about who benefits, and forces you to notice when a story serves
   237→no real user. The capability states the behavior. The so that clause is the most important and most often omitted:
   238→it captures the why, the underlying goal that §3.1 said you must reach past the solution to find. When a story’s
   239→benefit clause is vague or circular (“…so that I can flag patients”), it is a warning that no one understands why the
   240→feature exists — and a feature no one can justify is a feature to cut.
   241→To judge whether a story is ready to build, teams use the INVEST checklist.8 A good story is:
   242→I — Independent. It can be built and shipped without depending on other stories being done first.
   243→Dependencies make planning and prioritizing brittle.
   244→N — Negotiable. It is a starting point for a conversation, not a fixed contract. The details are worked out
   245→with the customer, not dictated by the card.
   246→V — Valuable. It delivers something a user or customer can perceive as valuable. “Refactor the database
   247→layer” is real work but is not a user story — it has no user-facing value on its own.
   248→E — Estimable. The team can roughly size it. If they can’t, the story hides too much uncertainty and needs
   249→to be split or researched first (a spike).
   250→S — Small. It fits comfortably within a single iteration. Big stories (“epics”) are split until each piece is
   251→small enough to finish and demo.
   252→T — Testable. You can state, in advance, how you will know it works. If you can’t write an acceptance test,
   253→you don’t yet understand the story well enough to build it.
   254→Principle. If a story fails INVEST, don’t force it into the sprint — fix the story. An unestimable story means
   255→you need a spike; an untestable one means the requirement is still ambiguous; a huge one means you should
   256→split it. INVEST is a diagnostic, not a bureaucratic gate.
   257→Attach acceptance criteria to each story — concrete conditions that must hold for the story to count as done.
   258→These are the seed of the tests you will write (Chapter 10) and the answer to “how will we know?”

   261→Given / When / Then: writing acceptance criteria as scenarios
   262→A widely used, highly readable form for acceptance criteria is Given / When / Then: you name the starting
   263→context (Given), the action or event (When), and the expected, observable outcome (Then). Each such triple is a
   264→scenario — one concrete example of the story working:
   265→Given a checked-in patient flagged for an interpreter, When the clinician opens the visit, Then an
   266→interpreter-needed banner is shown.
   267→This structured-English format is called Gherkin,9 and it comes from Behavior-Driven Development (BDD) —
   268→an approach that writes requirements as concrete examples of behavior, in language a customer can read.10 In a
   269→BDD workflow the scenarios live in plain .feature files and look like this:
   270→Feature: Interpreter alerts
   271→So that clinicians are prepared, staff want a visible interpreter flag.
   272→Scenario: Patient needs an interpreter
   273→Given a checked-in patient flagged for an interpreter
   274→When the clinician opens the visit
   275→Then an "interpreter needed" banner is shown
   276→Scenario: Patient does not need an interpreter
   277→Given a checked-in patient with no interpreter flag
   278→When the clinician opens the visit
   279→Then no interpreter banner is shown
   280→Why it matters. The same words that specify the requirement can run as an automated test. Every
   281→mainstream language has a BDD framework — the Cucumber family, among others — that maps each
   282→Given/When/Then step to code, turning the scenario into an executable specification.11 Write the scenarios
   283→with the customer, and they double as your acceptance tests (Chapter 10, §10.2.3).

   288→A few guidelines for good scenarios: keep them declarative (describe what, not which buttons to click); use one
   289→When per scenario (multiple actions usually means you have multiple scenarios); cover the happy path and the
   290→important alternatives (mirroring the alternative flows of a use case, Chapter 5); and reuse a shared Given with a

   294→Stories are small by design, which makes them wonderful for building and terrible for seeing the forest. A system
   295→feature is a larger, coherent capability that a stakeholder would recognize and value as a unit — “appointment
   296→reminders,” “insurance verification,” “waitlist management.” Features group related stories and give the product a
   297→shape that executives, marketers, and users can talk about.
   298→Good features share several properties:

   320→Independently valuable. Shipping the feature (even partially) delivers value on its own, so it can be

   327→planning and communication, while stories answer “what do we build this week?” for execution. Keeping both
   328→views lets you zoom between the roadmap and the sprint without losing either.
   329→3.4.3 Perspective on User Stories
   330→User stories are a tool, not a religion, and it is worth being honest about their limits. A story is deliberately
   331→incomplete — that is its virtue for conversation and its vice for anything requiring precision. Stories are excellent
   332→for the flexible, discoverable parts of a system. They are poor at capturing:
   333→Cross-cutting quality requirements. “The system shall never log patient identifiers” does not fit “as a…I
   334→want…so that.” Quality requirements often need their own explicit statements with fit criteria (§3.1.3).
   335→Complex flows. A multi-step interaction with branches and error cases overflows a story card. That is what
   336→scenarios (§3.5) are for.
   337→Regulatory or contractual precision. When wording is legally binding, a conversational placeholder is
   338→not enough; you need a specification (§3.2.3).
   339→The mature view is that stories are one instrument in an ensemble. Reach for them when the need is user-facing,
   340→discoverable, and best refined by conversation. Reach for features when you need a roadmap, scenarios when you
   341→need to show a flow, explicit quality requirements when you need a testable property, and a specification when the
   342→cost of ambiguity is unacceptable. Using the right form for each need is itself a skill.

   375→them. For most teams, that ceremony is wasted; the lightweight chain above, maintained through artifacts that
   376→already exist, gives you the two answers that matter.
   377→Pitfall. A traceability matrix maintained by hand, after the fact, is fiction within a month. Trace through
   378→artifacts that execute (scenarios that run as tests) and links that tools maintain for you (story keys in commit
   379→messages, pull requests that close backlog items). If keeping the trace requires a separate document nobody
   380→runs, the trace is already broken.
   381→3.5 Writing User-Experience Scenarios
   382→A scenario tells a concrete story of a specific person using the system to accomplish a goal, step by step, in a
   383→specific situation. Where a user story is a one-line promise, a scenario is a narrative — and that narrative form
   384→exposes the gaps, branches, and human realities that terse formats hide.
   385→3.5.1 Guidelines for User-Experience Scenarios
   386→Scenarios earn their keep because they are concrete. Abstract requirements (“the system supports check-in”) let
   387→everyone imagine a different thing; a scenario with a named person, a real goal, and specific steps forces the
   388→vagueness into the open, where you can argue about it productively. Good scenarios follow a few guidelines:
   389→Use a concrete persona, not “the user.” Give them a name, a role, a level of skill, and a motivation. “Kati,
   390→a new front-desk clerk two weeks into the job” behaves very differently from “an experienced office
   391→manager,” and the difference drives design.
   392→
   393→===== صفحة الكتاب 78 =====
   394→Start from a trigger and a goal. What situation begins the scenario, and what is the person trying to
   395→achieve? A scenario without a goal is just a click-by-click script.
   396→Walk the happy path first, then the exceptions. Write the smooth case, then deliberately branch: what if
   397→the patient isn’t in the system? what if the network drops mid-check-in? Exceptions are where most
   398→requirements are missing.
   399→Stay in the problem, mostly. A scenario should describe the experience and the need, leaning away from
   400→prescribing exact UI. Some interface detail is unavoidable and useful, but a scenario dictating pixel
   401→positions has stopped being a requirement and become a design.
   402→Include the person’s emotional and situational context. “Kati is being watched by an impatient patient
   403→and three people in line” is a requirement in disguise: it tells you the interaction must be fast and forgiving
   404→under stress.
   405→Scenarios pair naturally with stories: a scenario reveals the flow and the missing steps; each step then becomes one
   406→or more INVEST-able stories to build. Together they give you both the narrative whole and the buildable parts.
   407→3.5.2 A Medical Scenario Example
   408→Here is a scenario for the clinic scheduler, written to the guidelines above:
   409→Scenario: Dorothy checks in an arriving patient during the morning rush.
   410→Dorothy is an experienced front-desk clerk. It is 8:55 a.m., the waiting room is filling, and she has a line of
   411→five patients. Her goal is to check each one in quickly and make sure the clinician has what they need before
   412→the visit.
   413→Laurel arrives for a 9:00 appointment. Dorothy greets her and searches by last name. Two patients share it;
   414→she confirms the date of birth and selects the right one. The system shows Laurel’s appointment and a
   415→reminder that her insurance verification is pending. With one tap Dorothy marks her arrived; the assigned
   416→clinician’s screen updates, and the pending-insurance flag is passed along so the clinician isn’t surprised.
   417→Total time: under fifteen seconds. Dorothy moves to the next patient.
   418→Exception A — patient not found. The next arrival, Harper, does not appear in search. She has an
   419→appointment but her name was misspelled at booking. Dorothy needs to find her by phone number and
   420→correct the record without leaving the check-in flow, because the line is growing.
   421→Exception B — patient needs an interpreter. Behind her, a patient is flagged needs-interpreter. The
   422→system must surface this prominently at check-in and on the clinician’s screen, early enough that an
   423→interpreter can be arranged before the visit starts, not discovered inside the exam room.
   424→Read what this one scenario produced. The happy path yields stories about search, disambiguation by date of
   425→birth, one-tap arrival, and clinician notification. Exception A reveals a whole missing capability — correcting a
   426→mis-booked record inline — that no one had put in the backlog. Exception B surfaces a cross-cutting need
   427→
   428→===== صفحة الكتاب 79 =====
   429→(interpreter flags that travel with the patient) and a quality requirement (surfaced early enough to act). And the
   430→context — the growing line, the fifteen-second budget — is a performance requirement stated as human reality
   431→rather than as a number, which you then make testable. A single well-written scenario routinely uncovers more
   432→real requirements than a day of abstract brainstorming, because it refuses to let anything stay vague.
   433→3.5.3 Storyboards: Drawing the Scenario
   434→A scenario is a story told in prose; a storyboard is the same story told in pictures — a short sequence of rough
   435→sketches, one frame per step, showing what the user sees and does at each moment. Borrowed from film,13
   436→storyboarding earns its place in requirements work because a drawing forces decisions that a sentence lets you
   437→dodge: what is actually on the screen when Dorothy searches? Where does the pending-insurance flag appear?
   438→What does she tap? If you cannot draw the frame, you have not yet imagined the requirement.
   439→Storyboards at this stage are lo-fi (low fidelity) on purpose: boxes, arrows, stick figures, and hand lettering, drawn
   440→in minutes on paper or a whiteboard. The roughness is a feature twice over. It keeps you from sliding into visual
   441→design when the question is still what happens, and it signals to stakeholders that everything is still changeable —
   442→people freely correct a scribble but hesitate to criticize something that looks finished. This is the same right-
   443→fidelity discipline as the fat-marker sketches and breadboards of §6.1.2; a storyboard is essentially a breadboard
   444→with time running left to right.
   445→Technique — storyboard an exception. Include failure cases and difficult paths in the storyboard alongside
   446→the ideal demonstration path. Drawing §3.5.2’s Exception A frame by frame — Harper at the desk, the empty
   447→search result, Dorothy switching to phone-number lookup, the inline correction, the line still moving —
   448→immediately raises the questions that matter: where does the correction happen? does Dorothy lose her place
   449→in check-in? what does the system show the next patient while she fixes it? Each frame you cannot draw is a
   450→requirement you have not discovered yet.
   451→A practical rhythm for a team: write the scenario (§3.5.1), storyboard its key flows in four to eight frames, walk
   452→the storyboard with a real user while they narrate what they’d expect, then convert what you learned into stories
   453→and acceptance criteria (§3.4). The storyboard itself becomes a cheap, durable artifact — teams pin them up and
   454→check designs against them for the rest of the project.
   455→3.6 Clarifying User Goals
   456→Beneath every feature and story lies a goal — a state of the world a stakeholder wants to bring about. “Reduce
   457→patient no-shows,” “protect patient privacy,” “get patients seen faster.” Goals are the why that stories’ “so that”
   458→clauses gesture at, and working explicitly with goals is how you keep a backlog coherent, spot conflicts early, and
   459→know what to cut. If requirements are the bridge, goals are the far bank you are trying to reach.
   460→
   461→===== صفحة الكتاب 80 =====
   462→3.6.1 Properties of Goals
   463→Good goals share properties that make them useful for reasoning:
   464→A goal describes an end, not a means. “Send SMS reminders” is a means; “reduce no-shows” is the end.
   465→Stating the end keeps your options open — maybe email or a phone call serves the goal better for some
   466→patients.
   467→A goal can be satisfied to a degree. Unlike a functional requirement (done or not), many goals —
   468→especially quality goals like “fast” or “secure” — are matters of more or less. These are sometimes called
   469→soft goals, and you satisfy them by argument and measurement, not by a checkbox.14
   470→A goal is measurable, at least in principle. “Reduce no-shows to under 8%” can be checked against
   471→reality. A goal you cannot measure even in principle is a slogan, and it will not help you decide anything.
   472→A goal has an owner. Someone cares about it and can speak for it. Ownerless goals drift and get traded
   473→away silently.
   474→3.6.2 Asking Clarifying Questions
   475→Stakeholders state goals vaguely, and your job is to sharpen them without putting words in their mouth. A few
   476→reliable moves:
   477→Ask “why?” to go up. “Why do you want SMS reminders?” → “So fewer patients miss appointments.”
   478→Now you have the real goal, and reminders are just one candidate solution.
   479→Ask “how?” to go down. “How would we reduce no-shows?” → reminders, overbooking, waitlists,
   480→deposits. Now you have candidate sub-goals and features.
   481→Ask “how much?” to make it measurable. “How much would count as success?” turns “faster check-in”
   482→into “under fifteen seconds per patient.”
   483→Ask “for whom?” and “when?” to expose hidden context. “Fast — for the experienced clerk or the new
   484→one? During the morning rush or midday?”
   485→Ask “what would go wrong if we didn’t?” to test whether the goal is real. If nothing bad happens without
   486→it, it may not be a goal worth funding.
   487→These questions do more than clarify; the “why” and “how” chains literally build the goal hierarchy of the next
   488→section.
   489→3.6.3 Organizing Goals into Hierarchies
   490→Asking “why” moves you toward broader goals; asking “how” moves you toward narrower ones. Do this
   491→systematically and goals organize into a goal hierarchy (or goal tree): a high-level goal at the top, refined
   492→downward into sub-goals, and finally into concrete features and stories at the leaves.14 The tree makes the
   493→rationale of your backlog visible — every leaf traces up to a goal someone owns, and every goal traces down to
   494→work that serves it.
   495→
   496→===== صفحة الكتاب 81 =====
   497→The hierarchy is a working tool, not decoration. When someone proposes a feature, you ask which goal it serves;
   498→if it fits nowhere, either you have found a missing goal or an unjustified feature. When you must cut scope, you
   499→cut from the least important goal, which tells you which whole cluster of stories can go — a far better
   500→conversation than arguing story by story.
   501→3.6.4 Contributing and Conflicting Goals
   502→The hierarchy above is tidy, but real goals interact, and the interactions are where the hard decisions live. Two
   503→relationships matter most:
   504→
   505→===== صفحة الكتاب 82 =====
   506→Contribution. One goal helps another. A waitlist both reduces no-shows’ impact (empty slots get filled)
   507→and speeds throughput. Features that serve multiple goals are high-value and should rise in priority — you
   508→get more for the same cost.
   509→Conflict. One goal works against another. “Speed up check-in” pushes toward fewer confirmation steps;
   510→“protect patient privacy” and “avoid mis-identification” push toward more verification. You cannot
   511→maximize both. The tension is real — the stakeholders live with it — and the analysis has done its job by
   512→making it visible.
   513→The value of naming conflicts is that it turns an implicit, unwinnable tug-of-war into an explicit trade-off decision
   514→that the right person can make on the record. “We will require date-of-birth confirmation even though it adds three
   515→seconds, because a mis-identified patient is a safety event and the sponsor accepts the speed cost.” That sentence
   516→— a goal conflict, resolved deliberately, with an owner and a rationale — is worth more than any amount of vague
   517→agreement. Unresolved conflicts do not disappear; they get resolved by accident, in code, by whoever happens to
   518→write the feature, usually badly.
   519→Principle. Surface goal conflicts before you build, and resolve them explicitly with the stakeholder who
   520→owns the trade-off. A conflict resolved on purpose is a decision; a conflict resolved by default is a bug with a
   521→backstory.
   522→3.7 Identifying Security Attacks
   523→Everything so far has asked “what do users want the system to do?” Security requirements demand the opposite
   524→question: “what do adversaries want the system to do, and how do we stop them?” This inversion is why security
   525→
   526→===== صفحة الكتاب 83 =====
   527→is so often missed — it does not show up when you ask cooperative users what they need. You have to deliberately
   528→adopt the mindset of someone trying to break your system.
   529→3.7.1 Attack Trees: Think Like an Attacker
   530→An attack tree is a goal hierarchy for the enemy.15 The root is the attacker’s goal — “read a patient’s medical
   531→record without authorization.” The branches are the ways to achieve it, refined downward into ever more concrete
   532→attacks, exactly as a goal tree refines a user goal into stories. The power of the technique is that it forces
   533→systematic thinking: instead of imagining a random hack, you decompose the attacker’s goal and make sure every
   534→branch is defended.
   535→
   536→===== صفحة الكتاب 84 =====
   537→Read the leaves as a to-do list of defenses. Each one becomes a security requirement: enforce multi-factor auth
   538→(defeats B1/B2), expire and bind session tokens (B3), check authorization on every record access server-side
   539→(C1/C2), encrypt backups (D1), and keep identifiers out of logs (D2 — which you may recognize as a quality
   540→requirement from §3.1.3, now justified by a concrete threat). The tree connects each defense to the attack it stops,
   541→which is exactly the justification a skeptical sponsor asks for when you request time to build security.
   542→
   543→===== صفحة الكتاب 85 =====
   544→3.7.2 Finding Possible Attacks
   545→Building the tree is a skill, and a few systematic prompts help you find branches you would otherwise miss:
   546→Enumerate the assets. What is worth stealing, corrupting, or denying? Patient records, credentials,
   547→availability of the system itself. Each valuable asset is the root of a tree.
   548→Walk the trust boundaries. Every place data crosses from less-trusted to more-trusted — a browser to
   549→your API, a third-party integration, an admin console — is a place to attack. Draw them and ask what could
   550→cross that shouldn’t.
   551→Use a threat checklist. A common one groups threats as STRIDE: Spoofing (pretending to be someone),
   552→Tampering (altering data), Repudiation (denying an action), Information disclosure (leaking data), Denial
   553→of service (blocking access), and Elevation of privilege (gaining rights you shouldn’t have).16 Walking each
   554→category against each asset surfaces branches brainstorming misses.
   555→Include social and procedural weaknesses, not only flaws in code. The cheapest attack is often a
   556→convincing phone call to the front desk. Social engineering, weak password-reset flows, and over-broad
   557→admin rights are attack branches too.
   558→Prune by feasibility, then defend the rest. Not every leaf is worth defending equally. Estimate each
   559→attack’s cost to the attacker and damage to you, and invest where cheap attacks meet high damage first.
   560→Pitfall. Security is a set of requirements you discover by asking a different question, not a feature you add at
   561→the end. A team that only elicits from cooperative users risks shipping a system that does exactly what good
   562→people want — and exactly what bad people want, too. Build one attack tree per critical asset while you
   563→write your user stories, not after your first breach.
   564→Attack trees and STRIDE are where security requirements begin; Chapter 11 picks the thread back up on the
   565→engineering side, from the OWASP Top 10 to securing the open-source supply chain your system is built from.
   566→3.8 Conclusion
   567→Requirements are where a project decides whether it is solving a real problem, and this chapter has been a catalog
   568→of ways to make that decision well. You saw that a requirement is a bridge from a person’s need to a team’s action,
   569→and that the need is rarely handed over clean: it is tacit, partial, dressed as a solution, and always changing. The
   570→requirements cycle — elicit, analyze, specify, validate — is how you close the gap between believed and real
   571→needs, and it never fully terminates, because working software teaches users what they actually want.
   572→The techniques compound. You classify needs (stated, implied, latent) and elicit them by triangulating interviews,
   573→observation, prototypes, and data — because each method reaches needs the others cannot. You write them in the
   574→right form for each job: user stories with the as a…I want…so that template and the INVEST qualities for
   575→discoverable, user-facing behavior; features for the roadmap; scenarios when a concrete narrative is the only thing
   576→
   577→===== صفحة الكتاب 86 =====
   578→that exposes the missing steps; explicit, measurable statements for quality requirements. You clarify the why by
   579→building goal hierarchies, and — crucially — you surface conflicting goals so the right owner resolves the trade-
   580→off on purpose rather than by accident in the code. And you invert the whole exercise for security, using attack
   581→trees to ask what an adversary wants, because cooperative users will never tell you.
   582→None of this produces certainty, and it is not meant to. The purpose is to identify errors early, while correction is
   583→inexpensive, by investing a small amount of deliberate effort before committing to the wrong solution. This
   584→applies Chapter 1’s engineering discipline to deciding what the team should build — the most consequential
   585→question in the project. The next chapters take these requirements and turn them into plans, designs, and tests —
   586→but each of those inherits the quality of the understanding you build here.
