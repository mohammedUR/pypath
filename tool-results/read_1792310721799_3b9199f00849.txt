     3→     3→Chapter 1 — Introduction
     4→     4→Where we are. This opening chapter introduces the ideas that thread through the book. These include the
     5→     5→definition of software engineering, the challenges associated with requirements, the inherent complexity of
     6→     6→software, the inevitability of defects, and the trade-offs among project scope, cost, and time. Additionally,
     7→     7→the chapter addresses the importance of social responsibility for software professionals. Subsequent chapters
     8→     8→each address one or more of these foundational challenges.
     9→     9→Writing a program that works once, on your machine, for input you chose yourself, is programming. Software
    10→    10→engineering is what you need when other people depend on that program — when it must keep working next
    11→    11→year, after five other developers have changed it, on inputs no one anticipated, at a scale no single person can hold
    12→    12→in their head. Understanding the gap between those two activities is the subject of this book.
    13→    13→1.1 What Is Software Engineering?
    14→    14→1.1.1 A Working Definition
    15→    15→Definition. Software engineering is the disciplined application of principles, methods, and tools to build and
    16→    16→evolve software systems that are useful, correct enough, and economical to change — usually by a team,
    17→    17→usually under changing requirements, usually at a scale that defeats ad-hoc effort.
    18→    18→Three words in that definition carry a lot of weight:
    19→    19→Team. Most real software — and nearly all software at commercial scale — is built by groups.
    20→    20→Coordination, communication, and shared understanding become first-class engineering concerns, not
    21→    21→afterthoughts. This is why Chapter 2 is about process and why an entire team project runs alongside the
    22→    22→concepts (Appendix A).
    23→    23→Changing. Requirements are discovered, not handed down. They shift as users learn what they actually
    24→    24→want. Methods that assume a frozen specification tend to fail; this is why the industry moved toward
    25→    25→iterative, agile methods (Chapters 2–4).
    26→    26→Scale. Beyond a few tens of thousands of lines, hardly anyone understands the whole system in detail. We
    27→    27→manage that with design and architecture (Chapters 6–7): dividing the system so each part can be
    28→    28→understood, changed, and tested in relative isolation.
    29→    29→What separates software engineering from mere coding is the focus on the why behind the how. Any competent
    30→    30→developer can be told “use dependency injection here.” An engineer understands what problem it solves
    31→    31→

    33→    33→(coupling), when it helps, and when it is overkill along with the trade-offs — and can make the call on a design
    34→    34→they have never seen before.
    35→    35→1.1.2 A Tale of Two Companies
    36→    36→Consider two startups building the same product — a scheduling app for clinics.
    37→    37→Company A hires strong programmers and tells them to “just ship.” They skip written requirements (“we all know
    38→    38→what a calendar does”), skip tests (“we’ll test by using it”), and let the architecture emerge by accretion. For three
    39→    39→months they look faster than anyone: demos appear weekly. Then the trouble starts. A change to time-zone
    40→    40→handling breaks appointment reminders in a way no one notices for two weeks. Onboarding a fourth engineer
    41→    41→takes a month because only one person understands the booking logic. Every new feature has a chance of
    42→    42→resurrecting an old bug. Velocity collapses under the weight of the system’s own complexity.
    43→    43→Company B spends the first two weeks talking to clinic staff, writing a handful of user stories, sketching a
    44→    44→modular architecture, and setting up automated tests and code review. Their first demo comes a week later than
    45→    45→Company A’s. But by the third month the two teams look nothing alike: new engineers are productive in days,
    46→    46→changes are localized, and a failing test usually catches a regression before it reaches a user.
    47→    47→Neither team is smarter. The difference is engineering discipline: Company B paid a small, early, predictable cost
    48→    48→to avoid a large, late, unpredictable one. The rest of this book is, in effect, a catalog of the disciplines that make a
    49→    49→team behave like Company B — and an honest account of when each is worth its cost.
    50→    50→1.1.3 Where the Discipline Came From
    51→    51→The phrase “software engineering” owes its early currency to practitioners, not to a standards committee. In the
    52→    52→1960s, Margaret Hamilton led the team at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory that wrote the flight software for
    53→    53→the Apollo Guidance Computer — the code that flew astronauts to the Moon.1 At the time, programming was
    54→    54→widely seen as a clerical afterthought to the “real” engineering of hardware. Hamilton pushed the term software
    55→    55→engineering into use deliberately, to insist that building flight software deserved — and required — the same
    56→    56→rigor and respect as the aerospace engineering around it.2
    57→    57→Her team’s discipline paid off in the most public way imaginable. Minutes before the Apollo 11 lunar landing, the
    58→    58→guidance computer was flooded with unexpected input and began running out of capacity, throwing alarms.
    59→    59→Because the software had been engineered with priority scheduling — the ability to shed low-priority work and
    60→    60→keep the tasks that mattered most for landing — it recovered instead of crashing, and the landing proceeded.3 A
    61→    61→design decision made long before launch, against a failure no one could fully predict, saved the mission. It
    62→    62→remains one of the clearest early demonstrations of what disciplined software design can do for a project.
    63→    63→The rest of the industry was learning similar lessons. Through the 1960s, hardware performance improved on the
    64→    64→exponential curves we now describe with Moore’s Law,4 while large software projects ran chronically late, blew
    65→    65→
    66→    66→===== صفحة الكتاب 21 =====
    67→    67→their budgets, and failed outright in mission-critical settings — defense, aerospace, banking. Software, not
    68→    68→hardware, had become the bottleneck. In 1968, a NATO-sponsored conference in Garmisch, Germany gave the
    69→    69→problem a name — the software crisis — and proposed a remedy in its very title: the NATO Software Engineering
    70→    70→conference argued that software should be developed like an engineering discipline, with deliberate methods,
    71→    71→rather than by ad-hoc programming and heroics.5
    72→    72→What ultimately answered the crisis, though, was not a fixed body of rules like a building code. Software turned
    73→    73→out to be too changeable for that. The discipline eventually adopted an empirical approach modeled on
    74→    74→experimental science. Iterate in small steps, seek fast feedback, prefer incremental progress you can evaluate, run
    75→    75→experiments when you are unsure, and let measured evidence — not opinion or authority — settle the argument.
    76→    76→Modern writers such as Dave Farley frame the effective software engineer as this kind of scientist.6 Keep that
    77→    77→framing in mind in Chapter 2: every process you will learn about there — Scrum’s sprints, XP’s tests, the spiral’s
    78→    78→risk-driven loops — is an institutionalized version of the same iterate, measure, and adjust cycle.
    79→    79→1.2 The Requirements Challenge
    80→    80→The hardest part of many projects is not building the system right, but figuring out which system to build. Fred
    81→    81→Brooks, the author of The Mythical Man-Month, famously argued that the hardest single part of software work is
    82→    82→deciding precisely what to build, because it is the one part whose mistakes most cripple the result and are hardest
    83→    83→to fix later.7
    84→    84→1.2.1 Identifying Users and Requirements
    85→    85→A requirement is a statement of something the software must do or a quality it must have. The catch is that the
    86→    86→people who have the need are usually not the people who build the software, and they rarely state the need
    87→    87→precisely. Users describe solutions (“add a dropdown here”) when what they have is a problem (“I can’t tell which
    88→    88→appointments are unconfirmed”). Part of the engineer’s job is to dig past the requested solution to the underlying
    89→    89→problem.
    90→    90→Requirements also come from more than the obvious end user. A clinic scheduling app has:
    91→    91→End users (front-desk staff) who book appointments,
    92→    92→Secondary users (clinicians) who view schedules,
    93→    93→Sponsors who pay and care about cost and compliance,
    94→    94→Regulators whose rules (privacy, accessibility) are non-negotiable constraints.
    95→    95→Chapter 3 gives concrete techniques — user stories, features, scenarios, and goal hierarchies — for eliciting and
    96→    96→recording what all of these stakeholders actually need.

    99→    99→1.2.2 Dealing with Requirements Changes
   100→   100→Requirements change, and this is normal, not a failure. Users learn from early versions; markets move; regulations
   101→   101→update. Because requirements will change, engineers focus on reducing the cost of accommodating change.
   102→   102→Two broad answers appear throughout the book:
   103→   103→1. Process-level: work in short iterations so that less new work accumulates before feedback arrives, limiting
   104→   104→the likelihood and extent of rework and keeping the team aligned with its latest understanding rather than a
   105→   105→stale specification (Chapter 2).
   106→   106→2. Design-level: structure the system so that a likely change touches one module, not twenty (Chapter 6).
   107→   107→Principle. The cost of a change should be proportional to the size of the change, not to the size of the
   108→   108→system. Achieving that is the central goal of good process and good architecture alike.
   109→   109→1.3 Software Is Intrinsically Complex
   110→   110→1.3.1 Sources of Complexity
   111→   111→Some complexity is essential — it is inherent in the problem. A tax-preparation program is complicated because
   112→   112→tax law is complicated; no cleverness removes that. Other complexity is accidental — it comes from our tools,
   113→   113→our choices, and our mistakes: tangled dependencies, unclear names, duplicated logic, leaky abstractions.7
   114→   114→Good engineering cannot eliminate essential complexity, but it relentlessly attacks accidental complexity. Much of
   115→   115→this book is about that: clear requirements and specifications (Chapters 3–5), design and patterns that package
   116→   116→proven solutions (Chapters 6–7), reviews that catch needless complication (Chapter 9).
   117→   117→Software is also unusually complex among engineered artifacts for structural reasons:
   118→   118→It is invisible — you cannot see its shape, so you rely on models and diagrams.7
   119→   119→It is discrete — a one-character change can flip behavior completely; there is no “small error → small
   120→   120→effect” safety net that continuous physical systems enjoy.
   121→   121→It is changed constantly — a bridge is designed once; software is redesigned every sprint.
   122→   122→1.3.2 Architecture: Dealing with Program Complexity
   123→   123→The primary weapon against complexity is decomposition: split the system into parts small enough to understand,
   124→   124→with well-defined interfaces between them, so that you can reason about one part without holding the others in
   125→   125→your head. When the parts and their connections are chosen deliberately, we call the result the system’s
   126→   126→architecture (Chapters 6–7).
   127→   127→
   128→   128→===== صفحة الكتاب 23 =====
   129→   129→A good decomposition has high cohesion (each module does one thing well) and low coupling (modules depend
   130→   130→on each other as little as possible, and only through their interfaces). These two ideas — introduced here,
   131→   131→developed in Chapter 6 — are perhaps the most important in all of software design.
   132→   132→1.4 Defects Are Inevitable
   133→   133→Humans make mistakes; therefore software has defects. The engineering response is to accept this and build
   134→   134→multiple, overlapping nets that catch defects early, when they are cheap to fix.
   135→   135→1.4.1 Fix Faults to Avoid Failures
   136→   136→It helps to separate three ideas that everyday speech blurs into “bug”:
   137→   137→A mistake (or error) is a human action that produces an incorrect result — a developer misreads a spec.
   138→   138→A fault (the “defect” in the code) is the resulting flaw in the software — an off-by-one in a loop.
   139→   139→A failure is the observable event when the running system deviates from correct behavior — the report
   140→   140→prints the wrong total.
   141→   141→A fault is latent until some execution triggers it into a failure. This chain — mistake → fault → failure — explains
   142→   142→why we attack defects at every stage: better requirements and reviews reduce mistakes; static checking and
   143→   143→
   144→   144→===== صفحة الكتاب 24 =====
   145→   145→inspection find faults before they run; testing provokes failures in a safe setting before users do. No single
   146→   146→technique suffices, which is why Chapters 9 and 10 are complementary.
   147→   147→1.4.2 Introduction to Testing
   148→   148→Testing runs the software on chosen inputs and checks the outputs against what we expect. It cannot prove the
   149→   149→absence of defects — as Dijkstra, a pioneer in computing and mathematics, observed, testing shows the presence
   150→   150→of bugs, never their absence8 — but a well-designed test suite makes it unlikely that a serious fault survives, and it
   151→   151→makes change safe by catching regressions. Testing is important enough that Chapter 10 is devoted to it, including
   152→   152→how to decide when you have tested enough (coverage).
   153→   153→1.4.3 Black-Box and White-Box Testing
   154→   154→Two complementary viewpoints run through all of testing:
   155→   155→Black-box (specification-based) testing chooses inputs from the specification, ignoring the code. You test
   156→   156→what the system should do: valid and invalid inputs, boundaries, and equivalence classes of behavior. Its
   157→   157→strength is that it finds missing logic and stays valid when the code is rewritten.
   158→   158→White-box (structure-based) testing chooses inputs by looking at the code, aiming to exercise its
   159→   159→statements, branches, and paths. Its strength is finding code that the specification-based tests never reach.
   160→   160→Neither subsumes the other, and Chapter 10 shows how to combine them and how to measure their thoroughness
   161→   161→with coverage criteria.
   162→   162→1.5 Balancing Constraints: The Iron Triangle
   163→   163→Every project runs under three linked constraints:
   164→   164→Scope — how much it does (features, quality).
   165→   165→Cost — how much effort/money it consumes.
   166→   166→Time — when it must ship.
   167→   167→
   168→   168→===== صفحة الكتاب 25 =====
   169→   169→1.5.1 Scope. Cost. Time. Pick Any Two!
   170→   170→These constraints are coupled: you cannot freely fix all three. Want more scope at the same time? Pay more cost
   171→   171→(people, tools). Want to ship sooner without more cost? Cut scope. The classic quip — “good, fast, cheap: pick
   172→   172→two” — captures the reality that quality is not free, and that a manager who demands more scope, sooner, for less
   173→   173→is mistaking innumeracy for ambition.
   174→   174→Agile methods make a deliberate choice here: they fix time and cost (a fixed-length iteration with a fixed team)
   175→   175→and let scope flex — the aim is to ship something valuable on the date, even if not everything. Plan-driven
   176→   176→methods more often fix scope and flex time. Chapters 2 and 4 return to this trade-off with concrete estimation and
   177→   177→prioritization techniques (story points, MoSCoW, value/cost/risk). And modern software increasingly ships as a
   178→   178→continuously updated service rather than a boxed product — Chapter 14 examines how that reshapes the
   179→   179→triangle, when releasing becomes a routine decision instead of a once-a-year event.
   180→   180→Pitfall. Silently absorbing a scope increase by working nights (“crunch”) hides the trade-off instead of
   181→   181→resolving it. It borrows against future quality — the debt is repaid, with interest, as defects and burnout.
   182→   182→1.6 Social Responsibility
   183→   183→Software runs medical devices, cars, elections, and financial systems. Engineering decisions are therefore ethical
   184→   184→decisions, and “I was just implementing the spec” is not a defense. This section grounds that claim in three cases.
   185→   185→1.6.1 Case Study: The Volkswagen Emissions Scandal
   186→   186→In 2015, regulators discovered that Volkswagen diesel vehicles contained a defeat device: software that detected
   187→   187→when the car was being emissions-tested (from steering, speed, and time patterns) and switched to a cleaner,
   188→   188→
   189→   189→===== صفحة الكتاب 26 =====
   190→   190→lower-performance mode only during the test. On the road, the cars emitted many times the legal limit of nitrogen
   191→   191→oxides.9
   192→   192→The software worked exactly as designed — and that is the problem. This was no mere testing failure — it was a
   193→   193→requirement that should never have been implemented. Engineers wrote that code. A system can satisfy its
   194→   194→specification and still be ethically wrong. A professional must sometimes refuse to build what they are asked to
   195→   195→build.
   196→   196→1.6.2 The ACM Code
   197→   197→The profession has written norms. The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the joint
   198→   198→ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code of Ethics state, among other principles, that software engineers
   199→   199→shall act in the public interest, produce work of high quality, be honest about their work and its limitations, and
   200→   200→maintain competence.10 11 These are not decoration: they give an engineer a shared, citable basis for saying no,
   201→   201→and for raising concerns without it being merely personal opinion.
   202→   202→1.6.3 Case Study: The Therac-25 Accidents
   203→   203→Between 1985 and 1987, a radiation-therapy machine, the Therac-25, delivered massive radiation overdoses to at
   204→   204→least six patients, several fatally.12 The causes were a textbook of engineering failures: a race condition in
   205→   205→concurrent control software that surfaced only when an operator typed quickly; removal of the hardware
   206→   206→interlocks that earlier models used as a safety backstop, leaving software as the sole safeguard; reused code never
   207→   207→designed for this configuration; misleading error messages; and a development culture that assumed the software
   208→   208→was correct and dismissed early field reports.12
   209→   209→The Therac-25 is the canonical warning that in safety-critical systems, software defects kill, and that process,
   210→   210→testing, and honest incident response are matters of life and death — not bureaucratic overhead.
   211→   211→1.6.4 Lessons for Software Projects
   212→   212→Across these cases, recurring lessons:
   213→   213→1. Build the right thing, not only the thing right — challenge requirements that would cause harm to users
   214→   214→or the public. (Chapter 10 returns to this distinction as validation versus verification.)
   215→   215→2. Don’t rely on a single safeguard — defense in depth (interlocks and software checks; reviews and tests).
   216→   216→3. Take field reports seriously — a dismissed anomaly is a failure waiting to recur.
   217→   217→4. Concurrency and reuse are risk multipliers — treat them with extra rigor.
   218→   218→5. You are accountable for what you ship, regardless of who asked for it.

   221→   221→1.7 Conclusion
   222→   222→Software engineering exists because software is built by teams, for changing needs, at a scale and complexity that
   223→   223→defeat improvisation, with defects that are inevitable and sometimes dangerous. The rest of this book is organized
   224→   224→as a set of responses to exactly those pressures:
   225→   225→Process (Ch. 2) coordinates the team and embraces change.
   226→   226→Requirements (Ch. 3–5) discover and pin down what to build.
   227→   227→Design and architecture (Ch. 6–7) tame complexity so change stays cheap.
   228→   228→Version control (Ch. 8) gives every change a name, a history, and an owner — the substrate every later
   229→   229→discipline builds on.
   230→   230→Static checking and testing (Ch. 9–10) catch the inevitable defects early.
   231→   231→Security (Ch. 11) makes the software resist attack, from the code to the supply chain it is built from.
   232→   232→Metrics (Ch. 12) tell us, with evidence rather than opinion, how we are doing.
   233→   233→AI across the lifecycle (Ch. 13) re-examines every one of those disciplines as coding agents take over
   234→   234→more of the typing.
   235→   235→Delivery (Ch. 14) carries a verified change the last mile, into production, safely.
   236→   236→Maintenance and evolution (Ch. 15) keep the system alive and changeable through the years after launch
   237→   237→— where most engineering effort actually goes.
   238→   238→A team project (Appendix A) puts it all into practice.
   239→   239→A word about the code you will meet along the way. Concepts come first in this book and code second, but from
   240→   240→Chapter 2 onward nearly every code-shaped idea is also shown as a short, runnable example — and each example
   241→   241→appears in six languages — Python, Java, JavaScript, Go, Ruby, and TypeScript — plus a language-agnostic
   242→   242→Generic tab written in pseudocode, for when you want the idea without any one language’s syntax. They sit
   243→   243→behind a row of tabs. Pick your language once and every example on every page follows; you can also open any
   244→   244→page with ?lang=go (or java, javascript, python, ruby, typescript, generic) to pre-select one. The tabs run
   245→   245→alphabetically, so Generic leads the row; the Ruby tab is selected by default until you pick another — and the
   246→   246→examples are written to read cleanly even in a language you have never used.
   247→   247→The others are worth your attention even so, because some lessons only appear in the contrast: information hiding
   248→   248→looks different as a Java interface, a Go implicit interface, Ruby duck typing, and a TypeScript interface; the
   249→   249→same unit tests read differently in pytest, JUnit, node:test, go test, and Minitest — and each difference says
   250→   250→something about the language’s design. Every snippet is a real program, kept as a runnable file with a test in the
   251→   251→book’s repository and exercised by continuous integration, so the examples cannot silently rot. And when an
   252→   252→example is supposed to fail — a type fault the compiler rejects in Chapter 9 — the error message printed beside it
   253→   253→is the compiler’s actual output, not a paraphrase.
   254→   254→Keep the four cross-cutting principles in view as you read: software is complex; requirements change; defects are
   255→   255→inevitable; teams need coordination. Nearly every technique in this book earns its place by answering one of
