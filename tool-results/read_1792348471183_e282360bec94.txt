     3→Chapter 13 — Software Engineering in the Age of AI
     4→Where we are. Every previous chapter described a durable engineering discipline — process, requirements,
     5→design, checking, testing, metrics, teamwork. This chapter asks what happens to those disciplines when
     6→generative AI and coding agents can draft code, tests, requirements, and designs in seconds. We separate
     7→what genuinely changes from what stubbornly does not, walk the whole lifecycle stage by stage, look
     8→honestly at the evidence (which is more mixed than the marketing), read the o16g “Outcome Engineering”
     9→manifesto as a provocation for the agentic era, and end with the concrete practices now emerging to work
    10→this way — spec-driven development, agent instruction files (CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md), and loop
    11→engineering.
    12→The headline claim of the moment is that AI writes the code now. A more useful way to describe the change is that
    13→AI moves the engineering bottleneck. For decades the bottleneck was producing correct code fast enough. As
    14→producing gets cheap, the bottleneck moves to deciding what to build and proving it works — which are the very
    15→activities this book has been teaching. The engineer who internalized Chapters 1–12 is the person best positioned
    16→to direct the agents.
    17→Note — what will age here. This chapter mixes two kinds of content with very different half-lives. Named
    18→tools, model capabilities, benchmark and study results, prices, and specific agent behaviors are edition-
    19→specific: they describe early 2026 and will drift, so treat every product name and number as a dated
    20→snapshot. The durable content is the principles — verify before you trust, keep a human accountable for
    21→what ships, write specifications precise enough to execute, and preserve traceability from requirement to
    22→change. When this edition’s numbers age out, those principles are the part meant to remain.
    23→13.1 What AI Changes — and What It Doesn’t
    24→13.1.1 Essential vs. Accidental Complexity, Revisited
    25→Recall from Chapter 1 the split between essential complexity (inherent in the problem) and accidental complexity
    26→(from our tools and toil). Generative AI is a spectacular weapon against accidental complexity: boilerplate, glue
    27→code, format conversions, first drafts of tests, “how do I call this API” friction. It is far weaker against essential
    28→complexity: deciding what the system should actually do, resolving conflicting goals, choosing an architecture
    29→whose likely changes are cheap. That boundary is the single most important idea in this chapter. Delegate the toil;
    30→own the essence.

    33→13.1.2 The Four Pressures Still Hold
    34→The book’s four cross-cutting pressures do not go away — AI just shifts them:
    35→Software is complex. Agents can generate complexity faster than ever, including accidental complexity
    36→you did not ask for. Architecture (Chapters 6–7) matters more.
    37→Requirements change. AI lowers the cost of responding to change, which raises the premium on knowing
    38→which change is worth making (Chapters 3–4).
    39→Defects are inevitable. AI produces plausible-looking defects at scale. Reviews, static checking, and tests
    40→(Chapters 9–10) become the load-bearing walls.
    41→Teams need coordination. “Teams” now include non-human members. Coordinating a swarm of agents is
    42→a new and unsolved organizational problem (see §13.4).

    51→Does AI make developers faster? The honest answer is it depends, and the evidence is mixed enough to demand
    52→humility.
    53→Perceived gains are large — and the headline numbers are vendor-reported. In GitHub’s own 2022
    54→research, surveyed Copilot users (about 2,000) reported feeling more productive, and in the companion
    55→controlled experiment — 95 developers, one well-specified task, building an HTTP server — the Copilot
    56→
    57→===== صفحة الكتاب 346 =====
    58→group finished about 55% faster.1 2 These are honest but narrow measurements, reported by the vendor of
    59→the product being measured, of one task type at one moment in the tools’ evolution.
    60→Measured gains are smaller and sometimes negative. In a 2025 randomized controlled trial, METR had
    61→experienced open-source developers do real tasks in repositories they knew well. Developers predicted AI
    62→would make them ~24% faster; in fact they were about 19% slower with AI — and still believed they’d
    63→been faster afterward.3 The slowdown came largely from the overhead of reviewing and integrating
    64→generated output. Scope the finding as carefully as the researchers did: it describes that specific setting —
    65→experienced developers, mature and familiar repositories, high quality standards, early-2025 tools — not
    66→AI-assisted work in general, and tools have moved since.
    67→Principle. Perceived productivity is not measured productivity. On familiar, high-standards code, the time
    68→you save typing can be swamped by the time you spend verifying what the agent produced. Verification is
    69→the new bottleneck — a theme this whole chapter returns to.
    70→The lesson is not “AI is useless” — gains are real for boilerplate, unfamiliar APIs, greenfield prototypes, and well-
    71→specified narrow tasks. These results suggest that AI provides more leverage to people who can judge its
    72→output well. Measure its effect on delivered work instead of relying on the subjective feeling of moving faster, as
    73→discussed in Chapter 12.

    82→AI compresses many process activities: drafting sprint plans, summarizing standups, writing retro notes, triaging
    83→issues, generating first-pass estimates. Agents can now take a ticket and open a pull request unattended, which
    84→strains the assumptions of Scrum and XP — what is a “sprint” when a swarm can attempt fifty backlog items
    85→overnight?
    86→Fails at: judgment about value and sequencing; reading the human dynamics of a team; owning
    87→accountability for what ships.
    88→Human owns: the definition of done, the choice of what to build next, and responsibility. The agile
    89→principle “working software is the primary measure of progress” gets sharper, not softer — see the
    90→manifesto’s “verified reality” in §13.4.4
    91→13.2.2 Requirements (Chapter 3)
    92→Large language models are genuinely useful for elicitation and drafting: turning interview notes, tickets, and
    93→support logs into candidate user stories, acceptance criteria (Given/When/Then), and edge-case lists. Studies
    94→report LLM-drafted requirements that reviewers rate as complete, consistent first drafts — comparable to an
    95→entry-level engineer’s — produced far faster and cheaper.5 6
    96→Fails at: knowing what users actually need versus what is plausible; an LLM will cheerfully invent
    97→requirements that sound reasonable and are wrong (hallucinated requirements), and its fluency can
    98→manufacture false consensus.7
    99→Human owns: talking to real stakeholders, resolving conflicting goals (Chapter 3’s goal hierarchies), and
   100→validating that a story reflects a real need. Use AI to widen the net of candidate requirements; use humans
   101→to decide which are true.
   102→13.2.3 Estimation and Analysis (Chapter 4)
   103→AI can suggest story-point estimates from historical data and surface comparable past work. But recall why we
   104→estimate in Chapter 4: story points model human uncertainty and drive prioritization, not stopwatch time. When
   105→agents do the building, the estimation question shifts from “how many engineer-hours?” toward “how much
   106→compute/ budget, and is the outcome worth it?” — precisely the manifesto’s cost, not time reframing (§13.4).
   107→MoSCoW, value/cost/risk, and Kano analysis remain the right tools; the “cost” axis just gets a new currency.
   108→13.2.4 Use Cases (Chapter 5)
   109→Given a goal and a happy path, models are good at enumerating alternative flows and exception cases — exactly
   110→the tedious part of use-case writing that people skip. That coverage is valuable. But an agent cannot tell you which
   111→actor goals matter, and it tends to pad use cases with flows no user will ever take.

   116→13.2.5 Design and Architecture (Chapters 6–7)
   117→This is where the human-owned layer is thickest. AI can propose class structures, generate Architecture Decision
   118→Records (ADRs), suggest applicable patterns from Chapter 7, and even prototype competing designs to compare.
   119→Research on multi-agent systems that go from requirements to candidate architectures is active but early, and leans

   121→Fails at: the significant, expensive-to-change decisions that define architecture (Chapter 6); it has no stake
   122→in your five-year maintenance cost and cannot feel the pain of high coupling.
   123→Human owns: modularity, coupling/cohesion trade-offs, and choosing which likely changes to make
   124→cheap. Let AI draft and compare designs; keep the decision and its rationale human and written down.
   125→13.2.6 Static Checking and Code Review (Chapter 9)
   126→AI both helps and harms here, sharply. AI reviewers and smarter static analyzers catch real bugs, explain them,
   127→and suggest fixes.10 But a growing share of code under review is now itself AI-generated — by late 2024
   128→Google reported more than a quarter of its new code was — which inverts the problem from Chapter 9: the scarce
   129→resource, reviewer trust and attention, is now spent on machine output produced faster than humans can vet it.11
   130→Pitfall. A 2023 controlled study found developers with an AI assistant wrote less secure code while being
   131→more confident it was secure — a false sense of safety.12 In the AI era, static analysis and human review are
   132→the brakes that make the speed usable.
   133→Human owns: deciding intent and trust (does this change do what we meant?), and keeping review
   134→standards from eroding under volume. Precision/recall trade-offs for analyzers (Chapter 9) matter more as
   135→more code flows through them.
   136→13.2.7 Testing (Chapter 10)
   137→Testing is where agentic AI has advanced most measurably. Benchmarks like SWE-bench (fix a real GitHub
   138→issue) and SWT-bench (generate a bug-reproducing test) have driven rapid progress in automated test generation
   139→and program repair; as of 2025, agents resolve a substantial fraction of such benchmark issues end to end.13 14
   140→Treat those numbers with care, though: passing a benchmark’s tests is evidence, not proof, of a correct fix —
   141→studies of SWE-bench-style evaluation have found patches that pass the benchmark suite while failing developer-
   142→written tests or behaving differently from the true fix, so weak or incomplete test suites can inflate reported
   143→

   145→resolution rates (the oracle problem again, wearing a benchmark’s clothes).15 Models are also strong at
   146→generating unit tests, property-based tests, and boundary cases (Chapter 10’s black-box techniques).16
   147→But the deepest problem in testing survives untouched: the oracle problem. A generated test encodes what the
   148→model thinks correct behavior is — which may simply mirror a misunderstanding also baked into the generated
   149→code. Coverage numbers can look great while the tests assert the wrong thing. Apply the same rule to an AI-
   150→generated test as to a human-written one (§10.1.5): never trust a test you have never seen fail. Introduce or restore
   151→the defect it claims to detect, confirm it fails for the expected reason, and only then count its passing result as
   152→evidence.
   153→Chapter 10’s discount example makes the failure concrete: suppose the billing spec says half-cent prices round up,
   154→while the generated code trips a language-specific rounding or representation trap — named in each variant’s
   155→leading comment — and the generated test asserts whatever the code already returns.
   156→Coverage looked great; the oracle was wrong. The suite agrees with the code because both encode the same
   157→mistake, and only a reviewer who knows the billing spec can see that the last cent rounded the wrong way — each
   158→variant’s final print line shows the value the spec required.
   159→Human owns: the oracle — the specification of correct behavior — and the coverage criteria that decide
   160→when testing is enough (statement/branch/MC/DC, §§10.3–10.5). Generated tests are a starting point to be
   161→reviewed, not ground truth.

   175→13.2.9 Quality and Metrics (Chapter 12)
   176→AI forces a reckoning with what we measure. Lines of code and commit counts were always weak proxies; when
   177→a machine emits thousands of lines on request, they become actively misleading. One industry analysis reports
   178→that as AI assistance spread (2021→2024), the share of duplicated/cloned code rose while refactoring fell — a

   181→measure outcomes — defect-removal efficiency, customer-found defects, DORA delivery metrics (Chapter

   185→In your own project, treat agents as fast, tireless, over-confident junior teammates. Let them scaffold, draft tests,

   187→passing review, and a metric that moved. Record where you used AI and how you verified it; that provenance is
   188→part of honest engineering (and of your final report).

   219→13.4 Outcome Engineering: The o16g Manifesto
   220→In 2026, Cory Ondrejka (co-creator of Second Life; former engineering leader at Google and Meta; CTO of
   221→Onebrief) published the o16g manifesto — Outcome Engineering — arguing that agentic development demands a
   222→new frame.19 20 Its thesis: “It was never about the code.” Code is “the incantation transforming computation
   223→into magic,” a mechanism for delivering an idea. Once agents remove the constraint of human typing bandwidth,
   224→the manifesto argues, creation is limited by the cost of compute, not human capacity — and the profession should
   225→move “beyond software engineering” toward engineering outcomes.19
   226→Treat this as an emerging framing, not a settled body of practice: at this writing the manifesto is months old, its
   227→vocabulary is still contested, and no independent evidence yet shows teams shipping better outcomes by adopting
   228→it. Read it the way you would read the Agile Manifesto in 2001 — a position paper whose worth the next decade
   229→will decide.
   230→It is organized around four shifts and 16 principles in two parts.19 Whatever you make of its rhetoric, its
   231→principles map with surprising directness onto the disciplines in this book — which is itself an argument that the
   232→fundamentals endure.
   233→The four shifts: Creation not code. Cost not time. Capacity not backlog. Certainty not vibes.19
   234→Part I — The Goals (“superpowered creation”)
   235→1. The Voyage — Human Intent. Agents explore paths; humans choose the destination. Don’t abdicate
   236→vision to the machine. (This book: requirements and goals, Ch. 3.)

   238→rate of positive change delivered, not lines written. (Testing & metrics, Ch. 10, 12.)

   240→protocols for debate, decision, and delivery. (Process, Ch. 2.)

   245→6. The Map — No Wandering in the Dark. Never dispatch an agent without context; map the territory
   246→before building. (Architecture description, Ch. 6.)

   289→13.5 Spec-Driven Development
   290→The manifesto argues for encoding intent explicitly (“Code the Constitution”) and treating verified reality as the
   291→only truth. Spec-driven development (SDD) is the concrete practice that does exactly that. Its core move is an
   292→inversion: in ordinary coding the specification serves the code — a document you write to explain software that
   293→already exists, and that drifts out of date the moment you stop maintaining it. In spec-driven development the code
   294→serves the specification. The spec is the durable artifact; the code is a generated, replaceable output of it.21
   295→That inversion only became practical when a machine could regenerate the code cheaply. When typing was the
   296→bottleneck, rewriting an implementation to match a changed spec was expensive, so specs decayed. When an
   297→agent can regenerate the implementation in minutes, the spec can be the thing you maintain and the code the thing
   298→you regenerate — which is what Chapter 3 always wanted requirements to be.
   299→The workflow. Open-source toolkits such as GitHub’s Spec Kit structure SDD as a pipeline of phases with a
   300→human checkpoint between each, so the agent never runs ahead of a decision you have not made:21
   301→
   302→===== صفحة الكتاب 355 =====
   303→Each phase has one job, and you do not advance until its artifact is validated:
   304→Constitution — the project’s non-negotiable principles (language, testing bar, security rules, style). Written
   305→once, referenced by every later phase. This is Chapter 3’s non-functional requirements and Chapter 6’s
   306→constraints made machine-readable — the same idea the manifesto calls “Code the Constitution” (§13.4,
   307→P10).
   308→Specify — what to build and why, deliberately silent on the tech stack. This is user-story and acceptance-
   309→criteria work (Chapters 3, 5), often written as Given/When/Then scenarios so that “done” is executable, not
   310→a matter of taste.
   311→Plan — how: the architecture, stack, and interfaces the implementation will use (Chapters 6–7).
   312→Tasks — the plan decomposed into small, independently testable units, ordered by dependency: the vertical
   313→slices of Chapter 2.
   314→
   315→===== صفحة الكتاب 356 =====
   316→Implement — the agent writes code and tests against the tasks one slice at a time, so its context never fills
   317→with the whole system at once.
   318→Between phases sit human approval gates. Their point is not ceremony; it is that a wrong decision caught in the
   319→spec is cheap to fix and the same decision discovered in production is not — §13.1.3’s rising cost of a confident-
   320→but-wrong output, front-loaded to the cheapest place to catch it.
   321→Principle. In spec-driven development the specification is the source and the code is the build output. If the
   322→two disagree, the spec wins and you regenerate — which is only safe if the spec is precise enough to serve as
   323→a contract.
   324→Early adopters report several-fold higher first-pass success from agents on non-trivial tasks when a real spec
   325→precedes the code, versus prompting from a one-line description.21 Treat such figures as encouraging practitioner
   326→reports, not controlled measurements — but the mechanism is sound and familiar to this book: most agent
   327→failures are underspecification failures, and SDD front-loads the specification. The bdfinst agentic-dev-team
   328→project is a working template of the same shape — a /specs → /plan → /build → /pr loop in which acceptance-
   329→test, design, and UX critic agents challenge the plan before any code is written, and red-green-refactor discipline
   330→(Chapter 10) is enforced during the build.22
   331→The catch is the one §13.2.7 already raised about generated tests: a spec written carelessly encodes a
   332→misunderstanding just as fluently as careless code does — and now that misunderstanding is the source of truth.
   333→SDD moves the human’s effort to where it belongs, into writing an unambiguous specification. That does not
   334→remove the human from the work; it puts more weight on the part that was always the hardest.
   335→13.6 Context as Infrastructure: CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, and Skills
   336→Out of the box, an agent begins a session knowing nothing about your project: your conventions, your build
   337→command, which directories are off-limits, what “done” means on your team. You can paste that briefing into the
   338→prompt every time — and pay to re-explain it forever, to every agent and every session. Or you can write it down
   339→once, in a file the agent reads automatically at the start of every run. The playbook of loop engineering (§13.7)
   340→names the recurring cost of not doing so as intent debt: the price of explaining “what this project is, what the
   341→rules are, where the traps are” over and over.23
   342→A small standard has emerged for paying that debt down. An agent instructions file — CLAUDE.md for Claude
   343→Code, or the tool-neutral AGENTS.md — is a Markdown briefing checked into the repository and loaded at the start
   344→of every session.24 25 AGENTS.md was formalized as an open specification in 2025 (led by OpenAI with Google,
   345→Cursor, and others) and donated to the Linux Foundation’s Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025; by then
   346→more than 60,000 projects had adopted it and more than twenty agent tools could read it.24 It is deliberately just
   347→Markdown with no required structure — “a README for agents.”
   348→

   351→they need it, with no ambiguity tolerated. The practices that make one effective are the practices of good technical
   352→writing under a hard constraint — the file is read every session, so every line spends context budget:25
   353→High-signal only. Include what materially changes a decision — the build command, the test bar, the
   354→directories not to touch — not what the agent can infer by reading the code.
   355→Imperative, not aspirational. “Never use inline mocks; use the factories in test/factories/” beats “we
   356→generally try to avoid mocks.”
   357→Few load-bearing rules. If every rule is marked important, none is. Keep the list short enough that all of it
   358→survives a skim.
   359→Workflow constraints. Say when the agent should stop and ask a human — the checkpoint the manifesto
   360→and §13.7 both insist on.
   361→This is the manifesto’s “All the Context, Everywhere” (§13.4, P11) at the scale of one repository: context
   362→embedded in the infrastructure rather than retyped into each prompt. It is also the mechanism behind Principle 10

   402→never named, where a bare NEVER LOG CARD NUMBERS does not. That is this book’s house style, and it is also the
   403→most portable prompt-writing advice there is.

   405→MUST emphasis can read as noise to a different tool; a terse, verification-first AGENTS.md can under-specify
   406→for a model that leans on stated priorities. Keep the portable facts (commands, conventions, definition of
   407→done) in AGENTS.md; tune tone per tool, against that tool’s published guide.
   408→Skills push the idea one step further. Where an instructions file is standing context loaded every time, a skill is a
   409→named, reusable unit of project knowledge — “how we cut a release,” “how we triage failing CI” — that an agent
   410→invokes only when the task calls for it. A skill can be versioned, tested, and improved like any other artifact; a
   411→prompt pasted into a script rarely is. That distinction matters for the loops of the next section: a loop that discovers
   412→its own work should trigger a named skill, not a wall of instructions buried in a cron job that no one will ever
   413→update.
   414→Principle. Encode project intent once, in a file the agent reads automatically, and maintain it like code.
   415→Every convention you leave only in your head is a convention the agent will violate — confidently, and at
   416→scale.
   417→For your team project (Appendix A), an AGENTS.md (or CLAUDE.md) is a high-leverage first artifact: capture your
   418→stack, your test and lint commands, your definition of done, and your review rules. It is living documentation that
   419→agents obey and humans can read — and writing it forces the team to make its tacit conventions explicit, which is
   420→worth doing even if no agent ever reads it.
   421→13.7 Loop Engineering
   422→The rungs of §13.1.3 ran from autocomplete to autonomous agents. There is a further rung beyond the ones drawn
   423→there, and in 2026 it acquired a name. Within a single week that June, three practitioners independently described
   424→the same shift: Peter Steinberger argued that the relevant skill was no longer prompting agents but designing the
   425→loops that prompt them; Boris Cherny, who built Claude Code, said from the vendor side that his job was now to
   426→write the loops that drive the agent; and Addy Osmani wrote it up and named it loop engineering.23 Its one-line
   427→
   428→===== صفحة الكتاب 359 =====
   429→definition is deliberately unsettling: loop engineering is replacing yourself — designing the system that prompts
   430→the agent, so that you no longer feed it one instruction at a time.23
   431→Four layers. Loop engineering is the widest layer of a progression the industry backed into, each layer minding
   432→something larger than the one before it:
   433→Prompt engineering minds one exchange; context engineering minds one window (§13.6); harness engineering
   434→arms one run (which tools an agent may call, and what counts as finished); loop engineering minds the widest
   435→scope of all — the system that runs the harness again and again with no human hand between turns. The first three
   436→layers still assume a person at the keyboard, directing the agent line by line. Loop engineering removes that
   437→assumption: the practitioner moves from inside the loop to outside it, building the loop rather than being it.23
   438→This layer belongs in a discussion of engineering discipline because it increases the consequences of mistakes.
   439→As §13.1.3 noted, a confident but incorrect output becomes more costly as the system performs more actions
   440→before a person reviews it. A loop is, by construction, a machine for maximizing that distance — it can run all
   441→
   442→===== صفحة الكتاب 360 =====
   443→night, change code you never looked at, and feed its own output back as tomorrow’s input. Every discipline below
   444→exists to shorten the gap between a mistake and its discovery.
   445→13.7.1 The Anatomy of a Loop
   446→A useful loop does five things each turn, and drops any one of them at its peril:23
   447→Discovery — the loop finds its own work (reads failing CI, open issues, recent commits) rather than being
   448→handed a task list. Ideally this logic lives in a skill (§13.6), not a static prompt.
   449→Handoff — each unit of work goes to an agent in an isolated workspace. In git that means a separate
   450→worktree per task, so parallel agents editing the same repository do not overwrite one another.
   451→Verification — a separate agent, defaulting to skeptical, checks the work. This is the load-bearing move;
   452→§13.7.2 is about why.
   453→
   454→===== صفحة الكتاب 361 =====
   455→Persistence — results land somewhere that outlives the conversation: a pull request, a ticket, a state file on
   456→disk. The agent forgets when its context clears; the repository does not.
   457→Scheduling — an automation (a timer, a CI trigger, a chat reaction) makes the loop run again without
   458→anyone remembering to start it. This is what turns a script you run by hand into a loop that runs itself.
   459→Miss one move and the loop fails in a characteristic way. The anti-patterns are the shadows of the skipped
   460→moves:23
   461→Move
   462→skipped
   463→How the loop fails
   464→Verification
   465→The nodding loop — the agent grades its own work, approves it, and accumulates plausible
   466→mistakes at machine speed. The most common failure.
   467→Persistence
   468→The amnesiac loop — good work is done, then forgotten when context clears; the next run redoes
   469→it or collides with it.
   470→Scheduling
   471→The manual loop — five good moves that a human still has to remember to trigger, and eventually
   472→won’t.
   473→Discovery
   474→The blind loop — the human still hand-feeds work each morning, so the expensive part (deciding
   475→what to do) was never automated.
   476→Handoff
   477→The tangled loop — parallel agents share one working directory and their edits collide into a mess
   478→no one can untangle.
   479→13.7.2 The Generator and the Evaluator
   480→The hardest part of a loop is not getting an agent to do the work — it is building something that can say “no.” And
   481→the agent that wrote the code is the worst possible judge of it.
   482→Ask an agent to grade what it just produced and it tends to praise it, because the context in which the code was
   483→written is already full of the reasons it was written that way; looking back, the agent sees its own justification, not
   484→the result. Anthropic’s engineering team, building agents that code autonomously for hours, hit this directly:
   485→agents overrate their own output, especially on subjective work.28 Their fix — borrowed explicitly from
   486→generative adversarial networks (GANs) — was structural, not a matter of wording. You cannot reliably make
   487→an author self-critical, but you can hand the work to a different agent, with different instructions, that carries none
   488→of the first one’s self-persuasion:28
   489→
   490→===== صفحة الكتاب 362 =====
   491→Two refinements make the evaluator real. First, an evaluator should execute the software and inspect its
   492→observed behavior, not only read the code. Anthropic’s evaluator used browser automation to operate the live
   493→application, capture screenshots, and grade the result against calibrated criteria.28 Second, it should begin from
   494→skepticism: treat the code as unverified until a check demonstrates that it behaves correctly. This is the old
   495→maker–checker principle from banking — the person entering a large transfer and the person approving it must
   496→differ — applied to the stop condition of a loop.
   497→Pitfall. A loop whose only reviewer is the agent that wrote the code is a loop nodding at itself. Coverage
   498→numbers, green checkmarks, and confident self-assessment all rise together while real quality does not —
   499→the §13.2.7 oracle problem, now running unattended. The reliable fix is structural: a separate evaluator, on a
   500→different model where you can, that defaults to “no.”
   501→This is §13.4’s Principle 2 (“Verified Reality is the Only Truth”) and §13.3’s through-line, arriving from the
   502→practitioners’ side: when generation is nearly free, the scarce resource is judgment, and a loop’s judgment lives
   503→in its evaluator. A strong generator with a weak evaluator produces confident garbage, faster; a modest generator
   504→with a sharp evaluator produces slow, reliable progress — and only the second compounds safely over many turns.
   505→13.7.3 The Costs That Accrue Silently
   506→A loop that runs itself is also a loop that makes mistakes by itself, and the more cheerfully it runs, the more quietly
   507→it errs. Four costs build up with no alarm while the loop is running, and they reinforce one another:23
   508→Verification debt — merged, unverified output piling up in the gap between “it ran” and “it’s right,”
   509→waiting for one morning when it comes due all at once.
   510→Comprehension rot — the codebase grows faster than anyone’s understanding of it, because reading
   511→generated code is duller than writing it and the loop never stops to read. A bug in a corner no human has
   512→read tends to surface first as a production incident.
   513→Cognitive surrender — the gradual loss of willingness to examine or challenge the system’s output. As the
   514→loop becomes more reliable, users may stop forming an independent judgment.
   515→Token blowout — the only cost that hits the bill directly. An idle bug can spin all night, burning budget on
   516→rounds that produce nothing, unless a hard cap stops it.
   517→The defenses are inexpensive and familiar. Read a representative sample of the loop’s output each day to
   518→preserve comprehension. Set hard budget caps before unattended execution begins. Keep at least one human
   519→
   520→===== صفحة الكتاب 363 =====
   521→checkpoint where the loop pauses for review, so that people remain able and prepared to intervene.
   522→Case study — Stripe’s “Minions.” Stripe’s internal coding agents produce roughly 1,300 pull requests each
   523→week after being triggered by an emoji reaction in Slack.29 Their reliability comes from the constraints
   524→surrounding the harness, which is a fork of the open-source Goose. A deterministic orchestrator assembles
   525→context and runs hard-coded quality gates (a linter the agent cannot skip, then a commit step) before and
   526→after the probabilistic model does its part; anything a rule can decide is kept out of the model’s hands. Every
   527→one of those PRs is still reviewed by an engineer — the humans did not leave, their time moved from writing
   528→to reviewing. Reliability came from the discipline around the loop, not the size of the model in it.29
   529→13.7.4 Stay the Engineer
   530→The unsettling framing — “loop engineering is replacing yourself” — has a sharp corollary. A loop is a faithful
   531→multiplier of whatever its builder brings: give it sound judgment and it executes good decisions a hundredfold;
   532→give it a lapse and it executes the lapse a hundredfold, faithfully, without ever pausing to ask whether it was
   533→right.23 The same loop, built by two engineers, can end in opposite places — one who reads the code and holds a
   534→firm sense of direction uses it to move faster on work they have mastered; one who stops reading uses it to never
   535→have to understand again, and six months later is the gatekeeper of a machine they can no longer audit.
   536→That is the deskilling risk of §13.4 stated operationally, and it resolves the same way: the human review point is
   537→not a temporary scaffold to remove once the loop is trusted. It is the permanent feature that keeps the loop
   538→trustworthy, and the day it is removed is the day comprehension rot begins in earnest. Build the loop in a way that
   539→preserves your ability and responsibility to review its work.
   540→Principle. Stop prompting the agent; design the system that prompts it. But design that system so a human
   541→can always still say “no” — a loop can execute judgment, it cannot supply it.
   542→Start small. A first loop should be almost embarrassingly modest: one finding, discovered and handled end to end,
   543→on a timer, with a separate check and a budget cap. Add parallelism last, after the checks have caught real
   544→mistakes — the Stripe pipeline is the end of that road, hardened over years, not the place to begin.
   545→13.8 Principles for the AI-Augmented Engineer
   546→Synthesizing the evidence and the manifesto into working advice:
   547→1. Own intent and verification. Choose the destination and define “correct”; let agents explore paths. You
   548→are accountable for what ships (Chapter 1’s ethics do not delegate).
   549→2. Keep the fundamentals. You cannot safely accept what you cannot evaluate. Understand the design, the
   550→tests, and the metrics well enough to catch a confident wrong answer.
   551→
   552→===== صفحة الكتاب 364 =====
   553→3. Shift your effort up the stack. Spend the time AI frees on specifying (clear requirements, acceptance
   554→criteria, ADRs) and checking (reviews, coverage, metrics) — not on generating more code.
   555→4. Manage the new risks. Security defects, license/provenance of generated code, hallucination, over-trust,
   556→and skill atrophy are now first-class engineering concerns.
   557→5. Measure outcomes, not activity. Judge yourself and your agents by verified value delivered and defects
   558→escaped — never by lines or PR counts.
   559→6. Automate the loop, not the judgment. As agents begin to run themselves, encode your intent where they
   560→read it (§13.6), specify before you generate (§13.5), and keep a separate check and a human gate in every
   561→loop (§13.7). Speed is only safe behind verification you control.
   562→13.9 Conclusion
   563→AI does not repeal the four pressures of software engineering; it relocates their weight. Producing code gets
   564→cheaper, so deciding what to build and proving it works — the subject of every chapter in this book — becomes
   565→the differentiator. The disciplines you learned are promoted, not made quaint. Requirements are now what you
   566→hand the agent to build from. The specification becomes the constitution it works under. Tests and reviews are the
   567→layer that decides whether the volume of generated code can be trusted, and metrics are the evidence that a real
   568→outcome shipped rather than mere output.
   569→The o16g manifesto pushes this to its edge — it was never about the code — and whether or not you accept its
   570→every claim, it points where the profession may be heading: engineers who direct fleets of agents toward well-
   571→specified, rigorously verified outcomes. The practices are arriving to match — you write the spec, encode the
   572→constitution in a file the agents read, and design the loop that runs them — but in every one of them the human’s
   573→job is the same: own the intent, keep the check, and stay able to say “no.” That job is not less engineering. It is
   574→more of the hardest, most human parts of it.
