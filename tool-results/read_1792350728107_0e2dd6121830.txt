   432→  1572→2.5 Automatic Differentiation
   433→  1573→Recall from Section 2.4 that calculating derivatives is the crucial step in all the optimization
   434→  1574→algorithms that we will use to train deep networks. While the calculations are straightfor-
   435→  1575→ward, working them out by hand can be tedious and error-prone, and these issues only grow
   436→  1576→as our models become more complex.
   437→  1577→Fortunately all modern deep learning frameworks take this work off our plates by offering
   438→  1578→automatic differentiation (often shortened to autograd). As we pass data through each
   439→  1579→successive function, the framework builds a computational graph that tracks how each value
   440→  1580→depends on others. To calculate derivatives, automatic differentiation works backwards
   441→  1581→through this graph applying the chain rule. The computational algorithm for applying the
   442→  1582→chain rule in this fashion is called backpropagation.
   443→  1583→While autograd libraries have become a hot concern over the past decade, they have a
   444→  1584→long history. In fact the earliest references to autograd date back over half of a century
   445→  1585→(Wengert, 1964). The core ideas behind modern backpropagation date to a PhD thesis
   446→  1586→from 1980 (Speelpenning, 1980) and were further developed in the late 1980s (Griewank,
   447→  1587→1989). While backpropagation has become the default method for computing gradients,
   448→  1588→it is not the only option. For instance, the Julia programming language employs forward
   449→  1589→propagation (Revels et al., 2016). Before exploring methods, let’s first master the autograd
   450→  1590→package.

   638→  1778→2.6 Probability and Statistics
   639→  1779→One way or another, machine learning is all about uncertainty. In supervised learning, we
   640→  1780→want to predict something unknown (the target) given something known (the features). De-
   641→  1781→pending on our objective, we might attempt to predict the most likely value of the target.
   642→  1782→Or we might predict the value with the smallest expected distance from the target. And
   643→  1783→sometimes we wish not only to predict a specific value but to quantify our uncertainty. For
   644→  1784→example, given some features describing a patient, we might want to know how likely they
   645→  1785→are to suffer a heart attack in the next year. In unsupervised learning, we often care about
   646→  1786→uncertainty. To determine whether a set of measurements are anomalous, it helps to know
   647→  1787→how likely one is to observe values in a population of interest. Furthermore, in reinforce-
   648→  1788→ment learning, we wish to develop agents that act intelligently in various environments.
   649→  1789→This requires reasoning about how an environment might be expected to change and what
   650→  1790→rewards one might expect to encounter in response to each of the available actions.
   651→  1791→Probability is the mathematical field concerned with reasoning under uncertainty. Given a
   652→  1792→probabilistic model of some process, we can reason about the likelihood of various events.
   653→  1793→The use of probabilities to describe the frequencies of repeatable events (like coin tosses) is
   654→  1794→fairly uncontroversial. In fact, frequentist scholars adhere to an interpretation of probability
   655→  1795→that applies only to such repeatable events. By contrast Bayesian scholars use the language
   656→  1796→of probability more broadly to formalize reasoning under uncertainty. Bayesian probability
   657→  1797→is characterized by two unique features: (i) assigning degrees of belief to non-repeatable
   658→  1798→events, e.g., what is the probability that a dam will collapse?; and (ii) subjectivity. While
   659→  1799→Bayesian probability provides unambiguous rules for how one should update their beliefs in
   660→  1800→light of new evidence, it allows for different individuals to start off with different prior be-
   661→  1801→liefs. Statistics helps us to reason backwards, starting off with collection and organization
   662→  1802→of data and backing out to what inferences we might draw about the process that generated
   663→  1803→the data. Whenever we analyze a dataset, hunting for patterns that we hope might charac-
   664→  1804→terize a broader population, we are employing statistical thinking. Many courses, majors,
   665→  1805→theses, careers, departments, companies, and institutions have been devoted to the study of
   666→  1806→probability and statistics. While this section only scratches the surface, we will provide the
   667→  1807→foundation that you need to begin building models.
