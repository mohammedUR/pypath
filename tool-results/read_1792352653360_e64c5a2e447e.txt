    34→lying parameters applied at each step. While the standard connections are applied syn-
    35→chronously to propagate each layer’s activations to the subsequent layer at the same time
    36→step, the recurrent connections are dynamic, passing information across adjacent time steps.

    88→9.1 Working with Sequences
    89→Up until now, we have focused on models whose inputs consisted of a single feature vector
    90→x ∈R𝑑. The main change of perspective when developing models capable of processing
    91→sequences is that we now focus on inputs that consist of an ordered list of feature vec-
    92→tors x1, . . . , x𝑇, where each feature vector x𝑡is indexed by a time step 𝑡∈Z+ lying in
    93→R𝑑.
    94→Some datasets consist of a single massive sequence. Consider, for example, the extremely
    95→long streams of sensor readings that might be available to climate scientists. In such cases,
    96→we might create training datasets by randomly sampling subsequences of some predeter-
    97→mined length. More often, our data arrives as a collection of sequences. Consider the
    98→following examples: (i) a collection of documents, each represented as its own sequence of
    99→words, and each having its own length 𝑇𝑖; (ii) sequence representation of patient stays in the
   100→hospital, where each stay consists of a number of events and the sequence length depends
   101→roughly on the length of the stay.
   102→Previously, when dealing with individual inputs, we assumed that they were sampled inde-
   103→pendently from the same underlying distribution 𝑃(𝑋). While we still assume that entire
   104→sequences (e.g., entire documents or patient trajectories) are sampled independently, we
   105→cannot assume that the data arriving at each time step are independent of each other. For
   106→example, the words that likely to appear later in a document depend heavily on words oc-
   107→curring earlier in the document. The medicine a patient is likely to receive on the 10th day
   108→of a hospital visit depends heavily on what transpired in the previous nine days.
   109→This should come as no surprise. If we did not believe that the elements in a sequence
   110→were related, we would not have bothered to model them as a sequence in the first place.
   111→Consider the usefulness of the auto-fill features that are popular on search tools and modern
   112→email clients. They are useful precisely because it is often possible to predict (imperfectly,
   113→but better than random guessing) what the likely continuations of a sequence might be,
   114→given some initial prefix. For most sequence models, we do not require independence, or
   115→even stationarity, of our sequences. Instead, we require only that the sequences themselves
   116→are sampled from some fixed underlying distribution over entire sequences.
   117→This flexible approach allows for such phenomena as (i) documents looking significantly
   118→different at the beginning than at the end; or (ii) patient status evolving either towards recov-
   119→ery or towards death over the course of a hospital stay; or (iii) customer taste evolving in pre-
   120→dictable ways over the course of continued interaction with a recommender system.

   200→Sometimes, especially when working with language, we wish to estimate the joint probabil-
   201→ity of an entire sequence. This is a common task when working with sequences composed
   202→of discrete tokens, such as words. Generally, these estimated functions are called sequence
   203→models and for natural language data, they are called language models. The field of se-
   204→quence modeling has been driven so much by natural language processing, that we often
   205→describe sequence models as “language models”, even when dealing with non-language
   206→data. Language models prove useful for all sorts of reasons. Sometimes we want to evalu-
   207→ate the likelihood of sentences. For example, we might wish to compare the naturalness of
   208→two candidate outputs generated by a machine translation system or by a speech recognition

   214→right fashion by applying the chain rule of probability:

   256→You may be wondering why we represented the factorization of a text sequence 𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇)

   258→seemingly random order? In principle, there is nothing wrong with unfolding 𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇)
   259→in reverse order. The result is a valid factorization:
   260→𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇) = 𝑃(𝑥𝑇)
   261→1
   262→Ö
   263→𝑡=𝑇−1

   266→However, there are many reasons why factorizing text in the same direction in which we
   267→read it (left-to-right for most languages, but right-to-left for Arabic and Hebrew) is preferred
   268→for the task of language modeling. First, this is just a more natural direction for us to think
   269→about. After all we all read text every day, and this process is guided by our ability to
   270→anticipate which words and phrases are likely to come next. Just think of how many times
   271→you have completed someone else’s sentence. Thus, even if we had no other reason to prefer
   272→such in-order decodings, they would be useful if only because we have better intuitions for
   273→what should be likely when predicting in this order.
   274→Second, by factorizing in order, we can assign probabilities to arbitrarily long sequences
   275→using the same language model. To convert a probability over steps 1 through 𝑡into one that
   276→extends to word 𝑡+1 we simply multiply by the conditional probability of the additional to-
   277→ken given the previous ones: 𝑃(𝑥𝑡+1, . . . , 𝑥1) = 𝑃(𝑥𝑡, . . . , 𝑥1) · 𝑃(𝑥𝑡+1 | 𝑥𝑡, . . . , 𝑥1).

   279→bitrary other locations. While all orders of factorization are valid, they do not necessarily

   281→but for other kinds of data as well, e.g., when the data is causally structured. For example,

   400→There is quite a difference in difficulty between interpolation and extrapolation. Conse-

   402→i.e., never train on future data. Given this kind of data, sequence models require specialized
   403→statistical tools for estimation. Two popular choices are autoregressive models and latent-
   404→variable autoregressive models. For causal models (e.g., time going forward), estimating
   405→the forward direction is typically a lot easier than the reverse direction. For an observed

  1314→9.5.3 Gradient Clipping
  1315→While you are already used to thinking of neural networks as “deep” in the sense that many
  1316→layers separate the input and output even within a single time step, the length of the se-
  1317→quence introduces a new notion of depth. In addition to the passing through the network
  1318→in the input-to-output direction, inputs at the first time step must pass through a chain of 𝑇
  1319→layers along the time steps in order to influence the output of the model at the final time
  1320→step. Taking the backwards view, in each iteration, we backpropagate gradients through
  1321→time, resulting in a chain of matrix-products of length O(𝑇). As mentioned in Section 5.4,
  1322→this can result in numerical instability, causing the gradients either to explode or vanish,
  1323→depending on the properties of the weight matrices.
  1324→Dealing with vanishing and exploding gradients is a fundamental problem when designing
  1325→RNNs and has inspired some of the biggest advances in modern neural network architec-
  1326→tures. In the next chapter, we will talk about specialized architectures that were designed in
  1327→hopes of mitigating the vanishing gradient problem. However, even modern RNNs often
  1328→suffer from exploding gradients. One inelegant but ubiquitous solution is to simply clip the
  1329→gradients forcing the resulting “clipped” gradients to take smaller values.
  1330→Generally speaking, when optimizing some objective by gradient descent, we iteratively
  1331→update the parameter of interest, say a vector x, but pushing it in the direction of the negative
  1332→gradient g (in stochastic gradient descent, we calculate this gradient on a randomly sampled
  1333→minibatch). For example, with learning rate 𝜂> 0, each update takes the form x ←x−𝜂g.
  1334→Let’s further assume that the objective function 𝑓is sufficiently smooth. Formally, we say
  1335→that the objective is Lipschitz continuous with constant 𝐿, meaning that for any x and y,
  1336→we have
  1337→| 𝑓(x) −𝑓(y)| ≤𝐿∥x −y∥.
  1338→(9.5.1)
  1339→As you can see, when we update the parameter vector by subtracting 𝜂g, the change in
  1340→the value of the objective depends on the learning rate, the norm of the gradient and 𝐿as
  1341→follows:
  1342→| 𝑓(x) −𝑓(x −𝜂g)| ≤𝐿𝜂∥g∥.
  1343→(9.5.2)
  1344→In other words, the objective cannot change by more than 𝐿𝜂∥g∥. Having a small value for

  1350→speed at which we can reduce the value of the objective. On the bright side, this limits by
  1351→just how much we can go wrong in any one gradient step.
  1352→When we say that gradients explode, we mean that ∥g∥becomes excessively large. In this
  1353→worst case, we might do so much damage in a single gradient step that we could undo all
  1354→of the progress made over the course of thousands of training iterations. When gradients
  1355→can be so large, neural network training often diverges, failing to reduce the value of the
  1356→objective. At other times, training eventually converges but is unstable owing to massive
  1357→spikes in the loss.

  1359→has the advantage that we do not bias the updates. But what if we only rarely get large
  1360→gradients? This drastic move slows down our progress at all steps, just to deal with the rare
  1361→exploding gradient events. A popular alternative is to adopt a gradient clipping heuristic
  1362→projecting the gradients g onto a ball of some given radius 𝜃as follows:

  1405→Once a language model has been learned, we can use it not only to predict the next token
  1406→but to continue predicting each subsequent one, treating the previously predicted token as
  1407→though it were the next in the input. Sometimes we will just want to generate text as though
  1408→we were starting at the beginning of a document. However, it is often useful to condition the
  1409→language model on a user-supplied prefix. For example, if we were developing an autocom-
  1410→plete feature for a search engine or to assist users in writing emails, we would want to feed
  1411→in what they had written so far (the prefix), and then generate a likely continuation.
  1412→The following predict method generates a continuation, one character at a time, after
  1413→ingesting a user-provided prefix. When looping through the characters in prefix, we
  1414→keep passing the hidden state to the next time step but do not generate any output. This is
  1415→called the warm-up period. After ingesting the prefix, we are now ready to begin emitting
  1416→the subsequent characters, each of which will be fed back into the model as the input at the
  1417→next time step.
  1418→@d2l.add_to_class(RNNLMScratch)
  1419→#@save

  1422→for i in range(len(prefix) + num_preds - 1):
  1423→X = torch.tensor([[outputs[-1]]], device=device)
  1424→embs = self.one_hot(X)
  1425→rnn_outputs, state = self.rnn(embs, state)
  1426→if i < len(prefix) - 1:
  1427→# Warm-up period
  1428→outputs.append(vocab[prefix[i + 1]])
  1429→else:
  1430→# Predict num_preds steps
  1431→Y = self.output_layer(rnn_outputs)
  1432→outputs.append(int(Y.argmax(axis=2).reshape(1)))

  1553→9.7 Backpropagation Through Time
  1554→If you completed the exercises in Section 9.5, you would have seen that gradient clipping is
  1555→vital for preventing the occasional massive gradients from destabilizing training. We hinted
  1556→that the exploding gradients stem from backpropagating across long sequences. Before in-
  1557→troducing a slew of modern RNN architectures, let’s take a closer look at how backprop-
  1558→agation works in sequence models in mathematical detail. Hopefully, this discussion will
  1559→bring some precision to the notion of vanishing and exploding gradients. If you recall our
  1560→discussion of forward and backward propagation through computational graphs when we
  1561→introduced MLPs in Section 5.3, then forward propagation in RNNs should be relatively
  1562→straightforward. Applying backpropagation in RNNs is called backpropagation through
  1563→time (Werbos, 1990). This procedure requires us to expand (or unroll) the computational
  1564→graph of an RNN one time step at a time. The unrolled RNN is essentially a feedforward
  1565→neural network with the special property that the same parameters are repeated throughout
  1566→the unrolled network, appearing at each time step. Then, just as in any feedforward neural
  1567→network, we can apply the chain rule, backpropagating gradients through the unrolled net.
  1568→The gradient with respect to each parameter must be summed across all places that the pa-
  1569→rameter occurs in the unrolled net. Handling such weight tying should be familiar from our
  1570→chapters on convolutional neural networks.
  1571→Complications arise because sequences can be rather long. It is not unusual to work with
  1572→text sequences consisting of over a thousand tokens. Note that this poses problems both
  1573→from a computational (too much memory) and optimization (numerical instability) stand-
  1574→point. Input from the first step passes through over 1000 matrix products before arriving
  1575→at the output, and another 1000 matrix products are required to compute the gradient. We
  1576→now analyze what can go wrong and how to address it in practice.
