    35→chronously to propagate each layer’s activations to the subsequent layer at the same time

    88→9.1 Working with Sequences
    89→Up until now, we have focused on models whose inputs consisted of a single feature vector
    90→x ∈R𝑑. The main change of perspective when developing models capable of processing
    91→sequences is that we now focus on inputs that consist of an ordered list of feature vec-
    92→tors x1, . . . , x𝑇, where each feature vector x𝑡is indexed by a time step 𝑡∈Z+ lying in
    93→R𝑑.
    94→Some datasets consist of a single massive sequence. Consider, for example, the extremely
    95→long streams of sensor readings that might be available to climate scientists. In such cases,
    96→we might create training datasets by randomly sampling subsequences of some predeter-
    97→mined length. More often, our data arrives as a collection of sequences. Consider the
    98→following examples: (i) a collection of documents, each represented as its own sequence of
    99→words, and each having its own length 𝑇𝑖; (ii) sequence representation of patient stays in the
   100→hospital, where each stay consists of a number of events and the sequence length depends
   101→roughly on the length of the stay.
   102→Previously, when dealing with individual inputs, we assumed that they were sampled inde-
   103→pendently from the same underlying distribution 𝑃(𝑋). While we still assume that entire
   104→sequences (e.g., entire documents or patient trajectories) are sampled independently, we
   105→cannot assume that the data arriving at each time step are independent of each other. For
   106→example, the words that likely to appear later in a document depend heavily on words oc-
   107→curring earlier in the document. The medicine a patient is likely to receive on the 10th day
   108→of a hospital visit depends heavily on what transpired in the previous nine days.
   109→This should come as no surprise. If we did not believe that the elements in a sequence
   110→were related, we would not have bothered to model them as a sequence in the first place.
   111→Consider the usefulness of the auto-fill features that are popular on search tools and modern
   112→email clients. They are useful precisely because it is often possible to predict (imperfectly,
   113→but better than random guessing) what the likely continuations of a sequence might be,
   114→given some initial prefix. For most sequence models, we do not require independence, or
   115→even stationarity, of our sequences. Instead, we require only that the sequences themselves
   116→are sampled from some fixed underlying distribution over entire sequences.
   117→This flexible approach allows for such phenomena as (i) documents looking significantly
   118→different at the beginning than at the end; or (ii) patient status evolving either towards recov-
   119→ery or towards death over the course of a hospital stay; or (iii) customer taste evolving in pre-
   120→dictable ways over the course of continued interaction with a recommender system.

   200→Sometimes, especially when working with language, we wish to estimate the joint probabil-
   201→ity of an entire sequence. This is a common task when working with sequences composed
   202→of discrete tokens, such as words. Generally, these estimated functions are called sequence
   203→models and for natural language data, they are called language models. The field of se-
   204→quence modeling has been driven so much by natural language processing, that we often
   205→describe sequence models as “language models”, even when dealing with non-language
   206→data. Language models prove useful for all sorts of reasons. Sometimes we want to evalu-
   207→ate the likelihood of sentences. For example, we might wish to compare the naturalness of
   208→two candidate outputs generated by a machine translation system or by a speech recognition

   256→You may be wondering why we represented the factorization of a text sequence 𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇)

   258→seemingly random order? In principle, there is nothing wrong with unfolding 𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇)
   259→in reverse order. The result is a valid factorization:
   260→𝑃(𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑇) = 𝑃(𝑥𝑇)

   262→Ö

   266→However, there are many reasons why factorizing text in the same direction in which we
   267→read it (left-to-right for most languages, but right-to-left for Arabic and Hebrew) is preferred
   268→for the task of language modeling. First, this is just a more natural direction for us to think
   269→about. After all we all read text every day, and this process is guided by our ability to
   270→anticipate which words and phrases are likely to come next. Just think of how many times
   271→you have completed someone else’s sentence. Thus, even if we had no other reason to prefer
   272→such in-order decodings, they would be useful if only because we have better intuitions for
   273→what should be likely when predicting in this order.

   275→using the same language model. To convert a probability over steps 1 through 𝑡into one that

   277→ken given the previous ones: 𝑃(𝑥𝑡+1, . . . , 𝑥1) = 𝑃(𝑥𝑡, . . . , 𝑥1) · 𝑃(𝑥𝑡+1 | 𝑥𝑡, . . . , 𝑥1).

   399→9.1.5 Summary
   400→There is quite a difference in difficulty between interpolation and extrapolation. Conse-
   401→quently, if you have a sequence, always respect the temporal order of the data when training,
   402→i.e., never train on future data. Given this kind of data, sequence models require specialized
   403→statistical tools for estimation. Two popular choices are autoregressive models and latent-
   404→variable autoregressive models. For causal models (e.g., time going forward), estimating
   405→the forward direction is typically a lot easier than the reverse direction. For an observed
   406→sequence up to time step 𝑡, its predicted output at time step 𝑡+ 𝑘is the 𝑘-step-ahead predic-
   407→tion. As we predict further in time by increasing 𝑘, the errors accumulate and the quality
   408→of the prediction degrades, often dramatically.
