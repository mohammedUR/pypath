   992→So far, we have focused on how to tackle multiclass classification problems by training

   998→patterns, as assessed empirically on previously unseen data (the test set). High accuracy
   999→on the training set means nothing. Whenever each of our inputs is unique (and indeed this
  1000→is true for most high-dimensional datasets), we can attain perfect accuracy on the training

  1177→4.6.3 Statistical Learning Theory
  1178→Put simply, test sets are all that we really have, and yet this fact seems strangely unsatisfy-
  1179→ing. First, we seldom possess a true test set—unless we are the ones creating the dataset,
  1180→someone else has probably already evaluated their own classifier on our ostensible “test
  1181→set”. And even when we have first dibs, we soon find ourselves frustrated, wishing we
  1182→could evaluate our subsequent modeling attempts without the gnawing feeling that we can-
  1183→not trust our numbers. Moreover, even a true test set can only tell us post hoc whether a
  1184→classifier has in fact generalized to the population, not whether we have any reason to expect
  1185→a priori that it should generalize.
  1186→With these misgivings in mind, you might now be sufficiently primed to see the appeal of
  1187→statistical learning theory, the mathematical subfield of machine learning whose practi-
  1188→tioners aim to elucidate the fundamental principles that explain why/when models trained
  1189→on empirical data can/will generalize to unseen data. One of the primary aims of statistical
  1190→learning researchers has been to bound the generalization gap, relating the properties of the
  1191→model class to the number of samples in the dataset.
  1192→Learning theorists aim to bound the difference between the empirical error 𝜖S( 𝑓S) of a
  1193→learned classifier 𝑓S, both trained and evaluated on the training set S, and the true error
  1194→𝜖( 𝑓S) of that same classifier on the underlying population. This might look similar to
  1195→the evaluation problem that we just addressed but there is a major difference. Earlier, the
  1196→

  1198→152

  1200→classifier 𝑓was fixed and we only needed a dataset for evaluative purposes. And indeed,
  1201→any fixed classifier does generalize: its error on a (previously unseen) dataset is an unbiased
  1202→estimate of the population error. But what can we say when a classifier is trained and
  1203→evaluated on the same dataset? Can we ever be confident that the training error will be
  1204→close to the testing error?
  1205→Suppose that our learned classifier 𝑓S must be chosen from some pre-specified set of func-
  1206→tions F . Recall from our discussion of test sets that while it is easy to estimate the error of a
  1207→single classifier, things get hairy when we begin to consider collections of classifiers. Even
  1208→if the empirical error of any one (fixed) classifier will be close to its true error with high
  1209→probability, once we consider a collection of classifiers, we need to worry about the possi-
  1210→bility that just one of them will receive a badly estimated error. The worry is that we might
  1211→pick such a classifier and thereby grossly underestimate the population error. Moreover,
  1212→even for linear models, because their parameters are continuously valued, we are typically
  1213→choosing from an infinite class of functions (|F | = ∞).
  1214→One ambitious solution to the problem is to develop analytic tools for proving uniform
  1215→convergence, i.e., that with high probability, the empirical error rate for every classifier
  1216→in the class 𝑓∈F will simultaneously converge to its true error rate. In other words,
  1217→we seek a theoretical principle that would allow us to state that with probability at least
  1218→1 −𝛿(for some small 𝛿) no classifier’s error rate 𝜖( 𝑓) (among all classifiers in the class
  1219→F ) will be misestimated by more than some small amount 𝛼. Clearly, we cannot make
  1220→such statements for all model classes F . Recall the class of memorization machines that
  1221→always achieve empirical error 0 but never outperform random guessing on the underlying
  1222→population.
  1223→In a sense the class of memorizers is too flexible. No such a uniform convergence result
  1224→could possibly hold. On the other hand, a fixed classifier is useless—it generalizes perfectly,
  1225→but fits neither the training data nor the test data. The central question of learning has
  1226→thus historically been framed as a trade-off between more flexible (higher variance) model
  1227→classes that better fit the training data but risk overfitting, versus more rigid (higher bias)
  1228→model classes that generalize well but risk underfitting. A central question in learning

  1261→4.6.4 Summary
  1262→The most straightforward way to evaluate a model is to consult a test set comprised of pre-
  1263→viously unseen data. Test set evaluations provide an unbiased estimate of the true error
  1264→and converge at the desired O(1/√𝑛) rate as the test set grows. We can provide approx-
  1265→imate confidence intervals based on exact asymptotic distributions or valid finite sample
  1266→confidence intervals based on (more conservative) finite sample guarantees. Indeed test
  1267→set evaluation is the bedrock of modern machine learning research. However, test sets are
  1268→seldom true test sets (used by multiple researchers again and again). Once the same test set
  1269→is used to evaluate multiple models, controlling for false discovery can be difficult. This
  1270→can cause huge problems in theory. In practice, the significance of the problem depends on
  1271→the size of the holdout sets in question and whether they are merely being used to choose
  1272→hyperparameters or if they are leaking information more directly. Nevertheless, it is good
  1273→practice to curate real test sets (or multiple) and to be as conservative as possible about how
  1274→often they are used.
  1275→Hoping to provide a more satisfying solution, statistical learning theorists have developed
  1276→methods for guaranteeing uniform convergence over a model class. If indeed every model’s
  1277→empirical error simultaneously converges to its true error, then we are free to choose the
  1278→model that performs best, minimizing the training error, knowing that it too will perform
  1279→similarly well on the holdout data. Crucially, any one of such results must depend on some
  1280→property of the model class. Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chernovenkis introduced the VC
  1281→dimension, presenting uniform convergence results that hold for all models in a VC class.
  1282→The training errors for all models in the class are (simultaneously) guaranteed to be close
  1283→to their true errors, and guaranteed to grow even closer at O(1/√𝑛) rates. Following the
  1284→revolutionary discovery of VC dimension, numerous alternative complexity measures have
  1285→been proposed, each facilitating an analogous generalization guarantee. See Boucheron
  1286→et al. (2005) for a detailed discussion of several advanced ways of measuring function
  1287→complexity. Unfortunately, while these complexity measures have become broadly useful
  1288→tools in statistical theory, they turn out to be powerless (as straightforwardly applied) for
  1289→explaining why deep neural networks generalize. Deep neural networks often have millions
  1290→of parameters (or more), and can easily assign random labels to large collections of points.
  1291→Nevertheless, they generalize well on practical problems and, surprisingly, they often gen-

  1311→4.7 Environment and Distribution Shift
  1312→In the previous sections, we worked through a number of hands-on applications of machine
  1313→learning, fitting models to a variety of datasets. And yet, we never stopped to contemplate
  1314→either where data came from in the first place or what we ultimately plan to do with the
  1315→outputs from our models. Too often, machine learning developers in possession of data
  1316→rush to develop models without pausing to consider these fundamental issues.
  1317→Many failed machine learning deployments can be traced back to this failure. Sometimes
  1318→models appear to perform marvelously as measured by test set accuracy but fail catastroph-
  1319→ically in deployment when the distribution of data suddenly shifts. More insidiously, some-
  1320→times the very deployment of a model can be the catalyst that perturbs the data distribution.
  1321→Say, for example, that we trained a model to predict who will repay rather than default on a
  1322→loan, finding that an applicant’s choice of footwear was associated with the risk of default
  1323→(Oxfords indicate repayment, sneakers indicate default). We might be inclined thereafter

  1334→Environment and Distribution Shift
  1335→similar issues abound in many applications of machine learning: by introducing our model-
  1336→based decisions to the environment, we might break the model.
  1337→While we cannot possibly give these topics a complete treatment in one section, we aim here
  1338→to expose some common concerns, and to stimulate the critical thinking required to detect
  1339→such situations early, mitigate damage, and use machine learning responsibly. Some of the
  1340→solutions are simple (ask for the “right” data), some are technically difficult (implement a
  1341→reinforcement learning system), and others require that we step outside the realm of sta-
  1342→tistical prediction altogether and grapple with difficult philosophical questions concerning
  1343→the ethical application of algorithms.
  1344→4.7.1 Types of Distribution Shift
  1345→To begin, we stick with the passive prediction setting considering the various ways that data
  1346→distributions might shift and what might be done to salvage model performance. In one clas-
  1347→sic setup, we assume that our training data was sampled from some distribution 𝑝𝑆(x, 𝑦)
  1348→but that our test data will consist of unlabeled examples drawn from some different distri-
  1349→bution 𝑝𝑇(x, 𝑦). Already, we must confront a sobering reality. Absent any assumptions on
  1350→how 𝑝𝑆and 𝑝𝑇relate to each other, learning a robust classifier is impossible.
  1351→Consider a binary classification problem, where we wish to distinguish between dogs and
  1352→cats. If the distribution can shift in arbitrary ways, then our setup permits the pathological
  1353→case in which the distribution over inputs remains constant: 𝑝𝑆(x) = 𝑝𝑇(x), but the labels
  1354→are all flipped: 𝑝𝑆(𝑦| x) = 1 −𝑝𝑇(𝑦| x). In other words, if God can suddenly decide that
  1355→in the future all “cats” are now dogs and what we previously called “dogs” are now cats—
  1356→without any change in the distribution of inputs 𝑝(x), then we cannot possibly distinguish
  1357→this setting from one in which the distribution did not change at all.
  1358→Fortunately, under some restricted assumptions on the ways our data might change in the fu-
  1359→ture, principled algorithms can detect shift and sometimes even adapt on the fly, improving
  1360→on the accuracy of the original classifier.
  1361→Covariate Shift
  1362→Among categories of distribution shift, covariate shift may be the most widely studied.
  1363→Here, we assume that while the distribution of inputs may change over time, the labeling
  1364→function, i.e., the conditional distribution 𝑃(𝑦| x) does not change. Statisticians call this
  1365→covariate shift because the problem arises due to a shift in the distribution of the covari-
  1366→ates (features). While we can sometimes reason about distribution shift without invoking
  1367→causality, we note that covariate shift is the natural assumption to invoke in settings where
  1368→we believe that x causes 𝑦.

  1389→Label Shift
  1390→Label shift describes the converse problem. Here, we assume that the label marginal 𝑃(𝑦)
  1391→can change but the class-conditional distribution 𝑃(x | 𝑦) remains fixed across domains.
  1392→Label shift is a reasonable assumption to make when we believe that 𝑦causes x. For ex-
  1393→ample, we may want to predict diagnoses given their symptoms (or other manifestations),
  1394→even as the relative prevalence of diagnoses are changing over time. Label shift is the ap-
  1395→propriate assumption here because diseases cause symptoms. In some degenerate cases the
  1396→label shift and covariate shift assumptions can hold simultaneously. For example, when the
  1397→label is deterministic, the covariate shift assumption will be satisfied, even when 𝑦causes
  1398→x. Interestingly, in these cases, it is often advantageous to work with methods that flow
  1399→from the label shift assumption. That is because these methods tend to involve manipulat-
  1400→ing objects that look like labels (often low-dimensional), as opposed to objects that look
  1401→like inputs, which tend to be high-dimensional in deep learning.
