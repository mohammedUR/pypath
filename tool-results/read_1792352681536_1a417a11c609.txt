   204→  1177→4.6.3 Statistical Learning Theory
   205→  1178→Put simply, test sets are all that we really have, and yet this fact seems strangely unsatisfy-
   206→  1179→ing. First, we seldom possess a true test set—unless we are the ones creating the dataset,
   207→  1180→someone else has probably already evaluated their own classifier on our ostensible “test
   208→  1181→set”. And even when we have first dibs, we soon find ourselves frustrated, wishing we
   209→  1182→could evaluate our subsequent modeling attempts without the gnawing feeling that we can-
   210→  1183→not trust our numbers. Moreover, even a true test set can only tell us post hoc whether a
   211→  1184→classifier has in fact generalized to the population, not whether we have any reason to expect
   212→  1185→a priori that it should generalize.

   214→  1187→statistical learning theory, the mathematical subfield of machine learning whose practi-
   215→  1188→tioners aim to elucidate the fundamental principles that explain why/when models trained
   216→  1189→on empirical data can/will generalize to unseen data. One of the primary aims of statistical
   217→  1190→learning researchers has been to bound the generalization gap, relating the properties of the
   218→  1191→model class to the number of samples in the dataset.

   288→  1261→4.6.4 Summary
   289→  1262→The most straightforward way to evaluate a model is to consult a test set comprised of pre-
   290→  1263→viously unseen data. Test set evaluations provide an unbiased estimate of the true error
   291→  1264→and converge at the desired O(1/√𝑛) rate as the test set grows. We can provide approx-
   292→  1265→imate confidence intervals based on exact asymptotic distributions or valid finite sample
   293→  1266→confidence intervals based on (more conservative) finite sample guarantees. Indeed test
   294→  1267→set evaluation is the bedrock of modern machine learning research. However, test sets are
   295→  1268→seldom true test sets (used by multiple researchers again and again). Once the same test set
   296→  1269→is used to evaluate multiple models, controlling for false discovery can be difficult. This
   297→  1270→can cause huge problems in theory. In practice, the significance of the problem depends on
   298→  1271→the size of the holdout sets in question and whether they are merely being used to choose
   299→  1272→hyperparameters or if they are leaking information more directly. Nevertheless, it is good
   300→  1273→practice to curate real test sets (or multiple) and to be as conservative as possible about how
   301→  1274→often they are used.
   302→  1275→Hoping to provide a more satisfying solution, statistical learning theorists have developed
   303→  1276→methods for guaranteeing uniform convergence over a model class. If indeed every model’s
   304→  1277→empirical error simultaneously converges to its true error, then we are free to choose the
   305→  1278→model that performs best, minimizing the training error, knowing that it too will perform
   306→  1279→similarly well on the holdout data. Crucially, any one of such results must depend on some
   307→  1280→property of the model class. Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chernovenkis introduced the VC
   308→  1281→dimension, presenting uniform convergence results that hold for all models in a VC class.

   310→  1283→to their true errors, and guaranteed to grow even closer at O(1/√𝑛) rates. Following the
   311→  1284→revolutionary discovery of VC dimension, numerous alternative complexity measures have
   312→  1285→been proposed, each facilitating an analogous generalization guarantee. See Boucheron
   313→  1286→et al. (2005) for a detailed discussion of several advanced ways of measuring function
   314→  1287→complexity. Unfortunately, while these complexity measures have become broadly useful
   315→  1288→tools in statistical theory, they turn out to be powerless (as straightforwardly applied) for
   316→  1289→explaining why deep neural networks generalize. Deep neural networks often have millions
   317→  1290→of parameters (or more), and can easily assign random labels to large collections of points.
   318→  1291→Nevertheless, they generalize well on practical problems and, surprisingly, they often gen-

   338→  1311→4.7 Environment and Distribution Shift
   339→  1312→In the previous sections, we worked through a number of hands-on applications of machine
   340→  1313→learning, fitting models to a variety of datasets. And yet, we never stopped to contemplate
   341→  1314→either where data came from in the first place or what we ultimately plan to do with the
   342→  1315→outputs from our models. Too often, machine learning developers in possession of data
   343→  1316→rush to develop models without pausing to consider these fundamental issues.
   344→  1317→Many failed machine learning deployments can be traced back to this failure. Sometimes
   345→  1318→models appear to perform marvelously as measured by test set accuracy but fail catastroph-
   346→  1319→ically in deployment when the distribution of data suddenly shifts. More insidiously, some-
   347→  1320→times the very deployment of a model can be the catalyst that perturbs the data distribution.
   348→  1321→Say, for example, that we trained a model to predict who will repay rather than default on a
   349→  1322→loan, finding that an applicant’s choice of footwear was associated with the risk of default
   350→  1323→(Oxfords indicate repayment, sneakers indicate default). We might be inclined thereafter
   351→  1324→to grant a loan to any applicant wearing Oxfords and to deny all applicants wearing sneak-
   352→  1325→ers.
   353→  1326→In this case, our ill-considered leap from pattern recognition to decision-making and our
   354→  1327→failure to critically consider the environment might have disastrous consequences. For
   355→  1328→starters, as soon as we began making decisions based on footwear, customers would catch
   356→  1329→on and change their behavior. Before long, all applicants would be wearing Oxfords, with-
   357→  1330→out any coincident improvement in credit-worthiness. Take a minute to digest this because
   358→  1331→

   360→  1333→155
   361→  1334→Environment and Distribution Shift
   362→  1335→similar issues abound in many applications of machine learning: by introducing our model-
   363→  1336→based decisions to the environment, we might break the model.
   364→  1337→While we cannot possibly give these topics a complete treatment in one section, we aim here
   365→  1338→to expose some common concerns, and to stimulate the critical thinking required to detect
   366→  1339→such situations early, mitigate damage, and use machine learning responsibly. Some of the
   367→  1340→solutions are simple (ask for the “right” data), some are technically difficult (implement a
   368→  1341→reinforcement learning system), and others require that we step outside the realm of sta-
   369→  1342→tistical prediction altogether and grapple with difficult philosophical questions concerning
   370→  1343→the ethical application of algorithms.
   371→  1344→4.7.1 Types of Distribution Shift
   372→  1345→To begin, we stick with the passive prediction setting considering the various ways that data
   373→  1346→distributions might shift and what might be done to salvage model performance. In one clas-
   374→  1347→sic setup, we assume that our training data was sampled from some distribution 𝑝𝑆(x, 𝑦)
   375→  1348→but that our test data will consist of unlabeled examples drawn from some different distri-
   376→  1349→bution 𝑝𝑇(x, 𝑦). Already, we must confront a sobering reality. Absent any assumptions on
   377→  1350→how 𝑝𝑆and 𝑝𝑇relate to each other, learning a robust classifier is impossible.
   378→  1351→Consider a binary classification problem, where we wish to distinguish between dogs and
   379→  1352→cats. If the distribution can shift in arbitrary ways, then our setup permits the pathological
   380→  1353→case in which the distribution over inputs remains constant: 𝑝𝑆(x) = 𝑝𝑇(x), but the labels
   381→  1354→are all flipped: 𝑝𝑆(𝑦| x) = 1 −𝑝𝑇(𝑦| x). In other words, if God can suddenly decide that
   382→  1355→in the future all “cats” are now dogs and what we previously called “dogs” are now cats—
   383→  1356→without any change in the distribution of inputs 𝑝(x), then we cannot possibly distinguish
   384→  1357→this setting from one in which the distribution did not change at all.
   385→  1358→Fortunately, under some restricted assumptions on the ways our data might change in the fu-
   386→  1359→ture, principled algorithms can detect shift and sometimes even adapt on the fly, improving
   387→  1360→on the accuracy of the original classifier.
   388→  1361→Covariate Shift
   389→  1362→Among categories of distribution shift, covariate shift may be the most widely studied.
   390→  1363→Here, we assume that while the distribution of inputs may change over time, the labeling
   391→  1364→function, i.e., the conditional distribution 𝑃(𝑦| x) does not change. Statisticians call this
   392→  1365→covariate shift because the problem arises due to a shift in the distribution of the covari-
   393→  1366→ates (features). While we can sometimes reason about distribution shift without invoking
   394→  1367→causality, we note that covariate shift is the natural assumption to invoke in settings where
   395→  1368→we believe that x causes 𝑦.

   416→  1389→Label Shift
   417→  1390→Label shift describes the converse problem. Here, we assume that the label marginal 𝑃(𝑦)
   418→  1391→can change but the class-conditional distribution 𝑃(x | 𝑦) remains fixed across domains.
   419→  1392→Label shift is a reasonable assumption to make when we believe that 𝑦causes x. For ex-
   420→  1393→ample, we may want to predict diagnoses given their symptoms (or other manifestations),
   421→  1394→even as the relative prevalence of diagnoses are changing over time. Label shift is the ap-
   422→  1395→propriate assumption here because diseases cause symptoms. In some degenerate cases the
   423→  1396→label shift and covariate shift assumptions can hold simultaneously. For example, when the
   424→  1397→label is deterministic, the covariate shift assumption will be satisfied, even when 𝑦causes
   425→  1398→x. Interestingly, in these cases, it is often advantageous to work with methods that flow
   426→  1399→from the label shift assumption. That is because these methods tend to involve manipulat-
   427→  1400→ing objects that look like labels (often low-dimensional), as opposed to objects that look
   428→  1401→like inputs, which tend to be high-dimensional in deep learning.
