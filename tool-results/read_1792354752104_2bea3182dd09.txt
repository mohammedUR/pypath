   972→5.5 Generalization in Deep Learning
   973→In Chapter 3 and Chapter 4, we tackled regression and classification problems by fitting
   974→linear models to training data. In both cases, we provided practical algorithms for finding
   975→the parameters that maximized the likelihood of the observed training labels. And then,
   976→towards the end of each chapter, we recalled that fitting the training data was only an in-
   977→termediate goal. Our real quest all along was to discover general patterns on the basis
   978→of which we can make accurate predictions even on new examples drawn from the same
   979→underlying population. Machine learning researchers are consumers of optimization algo-
   980→rithms. Sometimes, we must even develop new optimization algorithms. But at the end
   981→of the day, optimization is merely a means to an end. At its core, machine learning is a
   982→statistical discipline and we wish to optimize training loss only insofar as some statistical
   983→principle (known or unknown) leads the resulting models to generalize beyond the training
   984→set.
   985→On the bright side, it turns out that deep neural networks trained by stochastic gradient de-
   986→scent generalize remarkably well across myriad prediction problems, spanning computer
   987→vision; natural language processing; time series data; recommender systems; electronic
   988→health records; protein folding; value function approximation in video games and board
   989→games; and numerous other domains. On the downside, if you were looking for a straight-
   990→forward account of either the optimization story (why we can fit them to training data) or
   991→the generalization story (why the resulting models generalize to unseen examples), then you
   992→might want to pour yourself a drink. While our procedures for optimizing linear models
   993→and the statistical properties of the solutions are both described well by a comprehensive
   994→body of theory, our understanding of deep learning still resembles the wild west on both
   995→fronts.
   996→Both the theory and practice of deep learning are rapidly evolving, with theorists adopting
   997→new strategies to explain what’s going on, even as practitioners continue to innovate at
   998→

  1000→190
  1001→Multilayer Perceptrons
  1002→a blistering pace, building arsenals of heuristics for training deep networks and a body of
  1003→intuitions and folk knowledge that provide guidance for deciding which techniques to apply
  1004→in which situations.
  1005→The summary of the present moment is that the theory of deep learning has produced
  1006→promising lines of attack and scattered fascinating results, but still appears far from a com-
  1007→prehensive account of both (i) why we are able to optimize neural networks and (ii) how
  1008→models learned by gradient descent manage to generalize so well, even on high-dimensional
  1009→tasks. However, in practice, (i) is seldom a problem (we can always find parameters that will
  1010→fit all of our training data) and thus understanding generalization is far the bigger problem.
  1011→On the other hand, even absent the comfort of a coherent scientific theory, practitioners
  1012→have developed a large collection of techniques that may help you to produce models that
  1013→generalize well in practice. While no pithy summary can possibly do justice to the vast
  1014→topic of generalization in deep learning, and while the overall state of research is far from
  1015→resolved, we hope, in this section, to present a broad overview of the state of research and
  1016→practice.
  1017→5.5.1 Revisiting Overfitting and Regularization
  1018→According to the “no free lunch” theorem of Wolpert and Macready (1995), any learn-
  1019→ing algorithm generalizes better on data with certain distributions, and worse with other
  1020→distributions. Thus, given a finite training set, a model relies on certain assumptions: to
  1021→achieve human-level performance it may be useful to identify inductive biases that reflect
  1022→how humans think about the world. Such inductive biases show preferences for solutions
  1023→with certain properties. For example, a deep MLP has an inductive bias towards building
  1024→up a complicated function by the composition of simpler functions.
  1025→With machine learning models encoding inductive biases, our approach to training them
  1026→typically consists of two phases: (i) fit the training data; and (ii) estimate the generalization
  1027→error (the true error on the underlying population) by evaluating the model on holdout data.
  1028→The difference between our fit on the training data and our fit on the test data is called the
  1029→generalization gap and when this is large, we say that our models overfit to the training data.
  1030→In extreme cases of overfitting, we might exactly fit the training data, even when the test
  1031→error remains significant. And in the classical view, the interpretation is that our models are
  1032→too complex, requiring that we either shrink the number of features, the number of nonzero
  1033→parameters learned, or the size of the parameters as quantified. Recall the plot of model
  1034→complexity compared with loss (Fig. 3.6.1) from Section 3.6.
  1035→However deep learning complicates this picture in counterintuitive ways. First, for classifi-
  1036→cation problems, our models are typically expressive enough to perfectly fit every training
  1037→example, even in datasets consisting of millions (Zhang et al., 2021). In the classical pic-
  1038→ture, we might think that this setting lies on the far right extreme of the model complexity
  1039→axis, and that any improvements in generalization error must come by way of regulariza-
  1040→tion, either by reducing the complexity of the model class, or by applying a penalty, severely
  1041→constraining the set of values that our parameters might take. But that is where things start
  1042→to get weird.
  1043→
  1044→===== صفحة الكتاب 231 =====
  1045→191
  1046→Generalization in Deep Learning
  1047→Strangely, for many deep learning tasks (e.g., image recognition and text classification) we
  1048→are typically choosing among model architectures, all of which can achieve arbitrarily low
  1049→training loss (and zero training error). Because all models under consideration achieve
  1050→zero training error, the only avenue for further gains is to reduce overfitting. Even stranger,
  1051→it is often the case that despite fitting the training data perfectly, we can actually reduce
  1052→the generalization error further by making the model even more expressive, e.g., adding
  1053→layers, nodes, or training for a larger number of epochs. Stranger yet, the pattern relating
  1054→the generalization gap to the complexity of the model (as captured, for example, in the depth
  1055→or width of the networks) can be non-monotonic, with greater complexity hurting at first
  1056→but subsequently helping in a so-called “double-descent” pattern (Nakkiran et al., 2021).
  1057→Thus the deep learning practitioner possesses a bag of tricks, some of which seemingly
  1058→restrict the model in some fashion and others that seemingly make it even more expressive,
  1059→and all of which, in some sense, are applied to mitigate overfitting.
  1060→Complicating things even further, while the guarantees provided by classical learning the-
  1061→ory can be conservative even for classical models, they appear powerless to explain why
  1062→it is that deep neural networks generalize in the first place. Because deep neural networks
  1063→are capable of fitting arbitrary labels even for large datasets, and despite the use of famil-
  1064→iar methods such as ℓ2 regularization, traditional complexity-based generalization bounds,
  1065→e.g., those based on the VC dimension or Rademacher complexity of a hypothesis class
  1066→cannot explain why neural networks generalize.
  1067→5.5.2 Inspiration from Nonparametrics
  1068→Approaching deep learning for the first time, it is tempting to think of them as parametric
  1069→models. After all, the models do have millions of parameters. When we update the models,
  1070→we update their parameters. When we save the models, we write their parameters to disk.
  1071→However, mathematics and computer science are riddled with counterintuitive changes of
  1072→perspective, and surprising isomorphisms between seemingly different problems. While
  1073→neural networks clearly have parameters, in some ways it can be more fruitful to think of
  1074→them as behaving like nonparametric models. So what precisely makes a model nonpara-
  1075→metric? While the name covers a diverse set of approaches, one common theme is that
  1076→nonparametric methods tend to have a level of complexity that grows as the amount of
  1077→available data grows.
  1078→Perhaps the simplest example of a nonparametric model is the 𝑘-nearest neighbor algorithm
  1079→(we will cover more nonparametric models later, for example in Section 11.2). Here, at
  1080→training time, the learner simply memorizes the dataset. Then, at prediction time, when
  1081→confronted with a new point x, the learner looks up the 𝑘nearest neighbors (the 𝑘points
  1082→x′
  1083→𝑖that minimize some distance 𝑑(x, x′
  1084→𝑖)). When 𝑘= 1, this algorithm is called 1-nearest
  1085→neighbors, and the algorithm will always achieve a training error of zero. That however,
  1086→does not mean that the algorithm will not generalize. In fact, it turns out that under some
  1087→mild conditions, the 1-nearest neighbor algorithm is consistent (eventually converging to
  1088→the optimal predictor).
  1089→Note that 1-nearest neighbor requires that we specify some distance function 𝑑, or equiva-
  1090→lently, that we specify some vector-valued basis function 𝜙(x) for featurizing our data. For

  1096→an optimal predictor, but different distance metrics 𝑑encode different inductive biases and
  1097→with a finite amount of available data will yield different predictors. Different choices of
  1098→the distance metric 𝑑represent different assumptions about the underlying patterns and the
  1099→performance of the different predictors will depend on how compatible the assumptions are
  1100→with the observed data.
  1101→In a sense, because neural networks are over-parametrized, possessing many more parame-
  1102→ters than are needed to fit the training data, they tend to interpolate the training data (fitting
  1103→it perfectly) and thus behave, in some ways, more like nonparametric models. More re-
  1104→cent theoretical research has established deep connection between large neural networks
  1105→and nonparametric methods, notably kernel methods. In particular, Jacot et al. (2018)
  1106→demonstrated that in the limit, as multilayer perceptrons with randomly initialized weights
  1107→grow infinitely wide, they become equivalent to (nonparametric) kernel methods for a spe-
  1108→cific choice of the kernel function (essentially, a distance function), which they call the
  1109→neural tangent kernel. While current neural tangent kernel models may not fully explain
  1110→the behavior of modern deep networks, their success as an analytical tool underscores the
  1111→usefulness of nonparametric modeling for understanding the behavior of over-parametrized
  1112→deep networks.
  1113→5.5.3 Early Stopping
  1114→While deep neural networks are capable of fitting arbitrary labels, even when labels are
  1115→assigned incorrectly or randomly (Zhang et al., 2021), this capability only emerges over
  1116→many iterations of training. A new line of work (Rolnick et al., 2017) has revealed that
  1117→in the setting of label noise, neural networks tend to fit cleanly labeled data first and only
  1118→subsequently to interpolate the mislabeled data. Moreover, it has been established that this
  1119→phenomenon translates directly into a guarantee on generalization: whenever a model has
  1120→fitted the cleanly labeled data but not randomly labeled examples included in the training
  1121→set, it has in fact generalized (Garg et al., 2021).
  1122→Together these findings help to motivate early stopping, a classic technique for regularizing
  1123→deep neural networks. Here, rather than directly constraining the values of the weights, one
  1124→constrains the number of epochs of training. The most common way to determine the
  1125→stopping criterion is to monitor validation error throughout training (typically by checking
  1126→once after each epoch) and to cut off training when the validation error has not decreased
  1127→by more than some small amount 𝜖for some number of epochs. This is sometimes called a
  1128→patience criterion. As well as the potential to lead to better generalization in the setting of
  1129→noisy labels, another benefit of early stopping is the time saved. Once the patience criterion
  1130→is met, one can terminate training. For large models that might require days of training
  1131→simultaneously across eight or more GPUs, well-tuned early stopping can save researchers
  1132→days of time and can save their employers many thousands of dollars.
  1133→Notably, when there is no label noise and datasets are realizable (the classes are truly sep-
  1134→arable, e.g., distinguishing cats from dogs), early stopping tends not to lead to significant
  1135→improvements in generalization. On the other hand, when there is label noise, or intrinsic
  1136→

  1138→193
  1139→Generalization in Deep Learning
  1140→variability in the label (e.g., predicting mortality among patients), early stopping is crucial.
  1141→Training models until they interpolate noisy data is typically a bad idea.
  1142→5.5.4 Classical Regularization Methods for Deep Networks
  1143→In Chapter 3, we described several classical regularization techniques for constraining the
  1144→complexity of our models. In particular, Section 3.7 introduced a method called weight
  1145→decay, which consists of adding a regularization term to the loss function in order to penalize
  1146→large values of the weights. Depending on which weight norm is penalized this technique
  1147→is known either as ridge regularization (for ℓ2 penalty) or lasso regularization (for an ℓ1
  1148→penalty). In the classical analysis of these regularizers, they are considered as sufficiently
  1149→restrictive on the values that the weights can take to prevent the model from fitting arbitrary
  1150→labels.
  1151→In deep learning implementations, weight decay remains a popular tool. However, re-
  1152→searchers have noted that typical strengths of ℓ2 regularization are insufficient to prevent the
  1153→networks from interpolating the data (Zhang et al., 2021) and thus the benefits if interpreted
  1154→as regularization might only make sense in combination with the early stopping criterion.
  1155→Absent early stopping, it is possible that just like the number of layers or number of nodes
  1156→(in deep learning) or the distance metric (in 1-nearest neighbor), these methods may lead to
  1157→better generalization not because they meaningfully constrain the power of the neural net-
  1158→work but rather because they somehow encode inductive biases that are better compatible
  1159→with the patterns found in datasets of interests. Thus, classical regularizers remain popular
  1160→in deep learning implementations, even if the theoretical rationale for their efficacy may be
  1161→radically different.
  1162→Notably, deep learning researchers have also built on techniques first popularized in classi-
  1163→cal regularization contexts, such as adding noise to model inputs. In the next section we will
  1164→introduce the famous dropout technique (invented by Srivastava et al. (2014)), which has
  1165→become a mainstay of deep learning, even as the theoretical basis for its efficacy remains
  1166→similarly mysterious.
  1167→5.5.5 Summary
  1168→Unlike classical linear models, which tend to have fewer parameters than examples, deep
  1169→networks tend to be over-parametrized, and for most tasks are capable of perfectly fitting
  1170→the training set. This interpolation regime challenges many hard fast-held intuitions. Func-
  1171→tionally, neural networks look like parametric models. But thinking of them as nonpara-
  1172→metric models can sometimes be a more reliable source of intuition. Because it is often
  1173→the case that all deep networks under consideration are capable of fitting all of the train-
  1174→ing labels, nearly all gains must come by mitigating overfitting (closing the generalization
  1175→gap). Paradoxically, the interventions that reduce the generalization gap sometimes appear
  1176→to increase model complexity and at other times appear to decrease complexity. However,
  1177→these methods seldom decrease complexity sufficiently for classical theory to explain the
  1178→generalization of deep networks, and why certain choices lead to improved generalization
  1179→remains for the most part a massive open question despite the concerted efforts of many
  1180→brilliant researchers.

  1195→5.6 Dropout
  1196→Let’s think briefly about what we expect from a good predictive model. We want it to pe-
  1197→form well on unseen data. Classical generalization theory suggests that to close the gap
  1198→between train and test performance, we should aim for a simple model. Simplicity can
  1199→come in the form of a small number of dimensions. We explored this when discussing the
  1200→monomial basis functions of linear models in Section 3.6. Additionally, as we saw when
  1201→discussing weight decay (ℓ2 regularization) in Section 3.7, the (inverse) norm of the param-
  1202→eters also represents a useful measure of simplicity. Another useful notion of simplicity
  1203→is smoothness, i.e., that the function should not be sensitive to small changes to its inputs.
  1204→For instance, when we classify images, we would expect that adding some random noise to
  1205→the pixels should be mostly harmless.
  1206→Bishop (1995) formalized this idea when he proved that training with input noise is equiva-
  1207→lent to Tikhonov regularization. This work drew a clear mathematical connection between
  1208→the requirement that a function be smooth (and thus simple), and the requirement that it be
  1209→resilient to perturbations in the input.
  1210→Then, Srivastava et al. (2014) developed a clever idea for how to apply Bishop’s idea to the
  1211→internal layers of a network, too. Their idea, called dropout, involves injecting noise while
  1212→computing each internal layer during forward propagation, and it has become a standard
  1213→technique for training neural networks. The method is called dropout because we literally
  1214→drop out some neurons during training. Throughout training, on each iteration, standard
  1215→dropout consists of zeroing out some fraction of the nodes in each layer before calculating
  1216→the subsequent layer.
  1217→To be clear, we are imposing our own narrative with the link to Bishop. The original pa-
  1218→per on dropout offers intuition through a surprising analogy to sexual reproduction. The
  1219→authors argue that neural network overfitting is characterized by a state in which each layer

  1275→to implement this is to first draw samples from the uniform distribution 𝑈[0, 1]. Then we
  1276→can keep those nodes for which the corresponding sample is greater than 𝑝, dropping the
  1277→rest.
