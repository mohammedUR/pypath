  1207→10.7.1 Teacher Forcing
  1208→While running the encoder on the input sequence is relatively straightforward, handling
  1209→the input and output of the decoder requires more care. The most common approach is
  1210→sometimes called teacher forcing. Here, the original target sequence (token labels) is fed

  1212→the original target sequence, excluding the final token, are concatenated as input to the

  1302→Given a target output sequence 𝑦1, 𝑦2, . . . , 𝑦𝑇′ for each time step 𝑡′ (we use 𝑡′ to differentiate
  1303→from the input sequence time steps), the decoder assigns a predicted probability to each
  1304→possible token occurring at step 𝑦𝑡′+1 conditioned upon the previous tokens in the target
  1305→𝑦1, . . . , 𝑦𝑡′ and the context variable c, i.e., 𝑃(𝑦𝑡′+1 | 𝑦1, . . . , 𝑦𝑡′, c).
  1306→To predict the subsequent token 𝑡′ + 1 in the target sequence, the RNN decoder takes the
  1307→previous step’s target token 𝑦𝑡′, the hidden RNN state from the previous time step s𝑡′−1,
  1308→and the context variable c as its input, and transforms them into the hidden state s𝑡′ at the
  1309→current time step. We can use a function 𝑔to express the transformation of the decoder’s
  1310→hidden layer:
  1311→s𝑡′ = 𝑔(𝑦𝑡′−1, c, s𝑡′−1).

  1313→After obtaining the hidden state of the decoder, we can use an output layer and the softmax
  1314→operation to compute the predictive distribution 𝑝(𝑦𝑡′+1 | 𝑦1, . . . , 𝑦𝑡′, c) over the subse-
  1315→quent output token 𝑡′ + 1.

  1386→10.7.5 Loss Function with Masking
  1387→At each time step, the decoder predicts a probability distribution for the output tokens.
  1388→As with language modeling, we can apply softmax to obtain the distribution and calculate
  1389→the cross-entropy loss for optimization. Recall from Section 10.5 that the special padding
  1390→tokens are appended to the end of sequences and so sequences of varying lengths can be
  1391→efficiently loaded in minibatches of the same shape. However, prediction of padding tokens
  1392→should be excluded from loss calculations. To this end, we can mask irrelevant entries
  1393→with zero values so that multiplication of any irrelevant prediction with zero equates to
  1394→zero.
  1395→@d2l.add_to_class(Seq2Seq)
  1396→def loss(self, Y_hat, Y):
  1397→l = super(Seq2Seq, self).loss(Y_hat, Y, averaged=False)
  1398→mask = (Y.reshape(-1) != self.tgt_pad).type(torch.float32)
  1399→return (l * mask).sum() / mask.sum()

  1634→10.8.2 Exhaustive Search
  1635→If the goal is to obtain the most likely sequence, we may consider using exhaustive search:
  1636→enumerate all the possible output sequences with their conditional probabilities, and then
  1637→output the one that scores the highest predicted probability.
  1638→While this would certainly give us what we desire, it would come at a prohibitive com-
  1639→putational cost of O(|Y|𝑇′), exponential in the sequence length and with an enormous
  1640→base given by the vocabulary size. For example, when |Y| = 10000 and 𝑇′ = 10, both
  1641→small numbers when compared with ones in real applications, we will need to evaluate
  1642→1000010 = 1040 sequences, which is already beyond the capabilities of any foreseeable
  1643→computers. On the other hand, the computational cost of greedy search is O(|Y| 𝑇′): mirac-
  1644→ulously cheap but far from optimal. For example, when |Y| = 10000 and 𝑇′ = 10, we only
  1645→need to evaluate 10000 × 10 = 105 sequences.
